After Long Beach issued a mandatory evacuation order Friday, Rachel was no longer a tropical storm on Sunday and had been downgraded to post-tropical status, the National Weather Service said. But the agency said coastal-flood risks persisted after the downgrade.

Rachel's surf damaged coastal homes and Long Beach's historic boardwalk. Long Beach evacuated some oceanfront residents, and inspectors red-tagged eight buildings as unsafe to enter; the buildings spanned 13 addresses, including apartments.

Coastal defences tested

A mandatory evacuation order issued Friday covered homes on Seaside Walk between 64th and 66th places on Long Beach's Peninsula.

Long Beach officials used timber, sandbags and heavy equipment to reinforce damaged areas.

After flooding affected the Balboa Peninsula, Newport Beach declared a local emergency and closed all its beaches and piers Saturday morning.

Officials deployed sand berms, pumps and tidal valves as flood-control measures. They urged people not to climb the berms, saying doing so could weaken them and reduce their ability to protect homes and infrastructure.

Authorities activated up to 18 pumps and used a network of 86 tidal valves designed to keep ocean surges from backing up into storm drains.

Coastal flood risks through Tuesday

Even after Rachel became post-tropical, the weather service expected additional high tides to bring minor to moderate coastal flooding through Tuesday. A coastal flood warning covered beaches from Malibu to San Diego.

  • Flooding and erosion: Forecast surf of up to 9 feet and tides above 7.5 feet were expected to produce the greatest coastal impacts. The agency said high tides and surf could erode beaches and flood coastal parking lots and pathways.
  • Water safety and small boats: The agency warned that large waves and rip currents were expected to increase drowning risk and could capsize small boats near shore.

Inland flooding risks remain

In Southern California, Rachel brought more than 6 inches of rain to parts of San Diego County, according to preliminary National Weather Service data. Rainfall also triggered flooding and a debris flow that blocked both directions of California 74 south of Palm Desert, the Los Angeles Times reported. The weather service said heavy rainfall and runoff could flood low-lying areas and raise rockslide and mudslide risks.

Las Vegas recorded 1.08 inches of rain, tying its October daily rainfall record, according to the weather service. Flooding near the Las Vegas Strip left water in streets and submerged a casino parking lot. The submerged casino parking lot was near the Strip.

After Rachel's remnants moved inland, the weather service issued flood watches across parts of Utah, Idaho and Arizona. Andrew Orrison said localized flash flooding remained possible in the Southwest after Rachel weakened.

The weather service said additional Western rainfall after Rachel was from a separate weather system.

Conditions behind the coastal flooding

According to the Los Angeles Times, Jonathan Warrick attributed the unusual coastal flooding to a season of elevated ocean conditions, high tides, a Kelvin wave and hurricane waves. Warrick described the combination of high tides, a Kelvin wave and hurricane waves as unprecedented. A Kelvin wave can raise background sea levels by perhaps 6 to 12 inches.

El Niño is the warm phase of a natural climate pattern known as the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, or ENSO. Daniel Swain cited El Niño, a marine heat wave and record-warm ocean temperatures as conditions conducive to Rachel's passage through or just offshore of the state at tropical-storm strength, the Times reported.

The Times reported that only two storms have passed through or just offshore of the state at tropical-storm strength in what it called the modern record.

Looking beyond this event, Warrick said similar coastal events might occur more often during the coming El Niño season and in future decades. He linked that possibility to a long El Niño season, potential winter storms and king tides, and continued sea-level rise.

Long Beach response and recovery

Mayor Rex Richardson said Long Beach's infrastructure was not designed for the storm conditions.

Two residents offered differing views of the city's response. Kellie Canning argued that Long Beach had not done enough to prevent water damage, saying water was undermining home and street foundations and that there was nothing to stop it.

Marianna Messina praised the city's immediate response, according to the Times. She said the city had pumped water from streets and removed a foot-thick layer of sand deposited by floodwater.

Long Beach City Manager Tom Modica expected a lengthy recovery and said the city would seek state assistance and potentially federal help.

Hot, dry conditions ahead

Looking to the next weather phase after the flooding concerns, the National Weather Service forecast that Southern California would shift later in the week to hot, dry and windy conditions, raising wildfire potential.
