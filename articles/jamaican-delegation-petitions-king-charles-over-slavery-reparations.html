Jamaican Delegation Petitions King Charles Over Slavery Reparations

Jamaica’s government delegation lodged a petition on slavery reparations with King Charles during its London visit. The petition seeks legal clarity rather than a direct demand for payment. It asks the King to refer three legal questions on Jamaican slavery to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council.

Under the Judicial Committee Act of 1833, the UK monarch has authority to refer legal matters to the Judicial Committee for an advisory opinion. Buckingham Palace says it worked with Jamaica’s governor-general to ensure the petition was lodged through the proper process. The Palace says lodging the petition is distinct from referring its questions to the Judicial Committee, and that the King has no personal discretion over a referral to the Privy Council and must act on advice. Mirror News later reported that King Charles had referred the petition to the Privy Council for legal advice. The reported referral to the Judicial Committee has not been confirmed.

The three questions concern the lawfulness of enslavement, the relevance of international law and whether Britain has a duty to provide redress. Olivia Grange, Jamaica’s culture minister, says an opinion from the committee could enable the next stage of the country’s reparations claim and potentially lead toward a settlement. Jamaican officials believe such an opinion would not compel the UK government to pay reparations, but could shape later negotiations and legal options.

Constitutional expert Craig Prescott expects the petition to reach the Judicial Committee, but predicts that its members may view the unusual procedure sceptically. He attributes that caution to the procedure’s departure from ordinary legal channels, not to any claim that slavery was not wrong.

Separate artefact discussions

The delegation’s London programme also included discussions with the British Museum about returning cultural artefacts taken from Jamaica. Grange considers the objects sought to be priceless and central to Jamaica’s story. The artefact discussions are separate from the petition.

Arguments over redress

Grange says legal recognition could help bring a long-delayed settlement addressing the abuse suffered by Jamaican ancestors who were subjected to slavery. She argues that Britain should pursue a remedy because public funds continued to repay compensation for slave owners until 2015, while people who were enslaved and treated as property received no redress. She cites that timing as part of her argument for a present-day remedy. She presents this as both a legal and moral case. Grange suggested that some UK taxpayers may object, but said she believed a majority may think answers are required.

Jamaican reparations advocates argue that slavery’s legacy continues to cause underdevelopment and economic disadvantage in Jamaica and other former colonies. Grange argues that inherited systems continue to affect education, health, diet and Jamaica’s ability to meet current challenges. Ralph Gonsalves has similarly linked present underdevelopment to the combined legacy of enslavement and the genocide of indigenous peoples.

The UK government says it will not pay slavery reparations. James Kariuki, the UK representative to the United Nations, argues there is no obligation to provide reparations for historical conduct that was not a breach of international law when it occurred. Kariuki’s reasoning is that the law in force at the time applies; it challenges the proposed inquiry into a historical legal duty. Olivette Otele forecasts that the Labour-led UK government is unlikely to answer Jamaica favourably.

Separately, Buckingham Palace says King Charles is personally committed to greater understanding of slavery and to finding ways to address historic wrongs. That statement does not amount to support for a reparations settlement; the UK government’s stated position is that it will not pay reparations.

Political implications and regional demands

Prescott says rejection of the procedure could increase support for Jamaica becoming a republic. Otele says the petition’s legal approach could encourage other Commonwealth countries to make comparable petitions.

In a separate regional campaign, the Bahamas National Reparations Committee believes King Charles should apologise and that the monarchy should provide compensation for slavery’s continuing legacy. The committee argues that the Crown was slavery’s greatest beneficiary and says a royal visit without an apology would be unacceptable because the economic and social consequences remain visible.
