Brazil's left-wing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won a fourth term in the Oct. 25 runoff, beating right-wing rival Flávio Bolsonaro by 51.8% to 48.2%, but Bolsonaro refused to concede and said he would seek to annul the result.

Brazil's Superior Electoral Court confirmed the result on Sunday night, giving Lula a margin of nearly 4.4 million votes. Bolsonaro, a senator and the eldest son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, had won the first round on Oct. 4, taking 47% to Lula's 45%.

Lula is the first candidate since Brazil restored direct elections in 1989 to win a runoff after finishing second in the first round. His victory also ended a seven-election winning streak in Latin America over 18 months for right-wing candidates allied with U.S. President Donald Trump.

Bolsonaro's challenge targets a two-hour extension of voting ordered by Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin. Both campaigns had asked for more time after bad weather and transport problems disrupted polling. Bolsonaro said the decision "changed the rules in the middle of the game." Kássio Nunes Marques, president of the Superior Electoral Court, said the extension did not affect the result.

On Monday, Bolsonaro's Liberal Party said it accepted the outcome. "We recognize the presidential election results," party head Valdemar Costa Neto said. "But we believe the issues raised need to be properly clarified."

The same day, Trump said, without presenting evidence: "I didn't take sides, but it looked like another rigged election, frankly." Leaders across the political spectrum recognized Lula's win, including Colombia's president, Abelardo de la Espriella, who had backed Bolsonaro.

After the result, the real, Brazil's currency, fell about 12% against the dollar, and a 10% drop in the Bovespa stock index triggered an automatic 30-minute trading halt. On Monday, Brazil's Treasury canceled government bond auctions planned for Oct. 27 and Oct. 29.

"This was a defeat for denialism and lies," Lula said as he addressed supporters, referring to his opponent. "No one is required to like the president-elect, but he must govern for everyone," he said.

Lula, a former metalworker and union leader, first won the presidency in 2002. He spent 580 days in prison on corruption convictions later annulled by the Supreme Court, then beat Jair Bolsonaro in 2022. Jair Bolsonaro is serving a 27-year sentence at home after being convicted of plotting a coup following that loss.

What each side says about the extension

Bolsonaro said the late change undermined equality between voters in different cities and states. He also said Zanin, whom he called a former lawyer for Lula, does not sit on the electoral court. "There's a lot that needs to be cleared up before Brazil simply accepts this foul play that took place in these elections," he said.

Zanin's order came three minutes before polls were due to close, and some polling stations reportedly learned of it only after they had shut.

Bolsonaro's own campaign had sought extra time because of heavy rain in southern Brazil, while Lula's cited bus problems in São Paulo and Salvador. Only about 34,000 votes were cast after the original closing time, roughly 0.03% of the total and far short of Lula's margin.

The dispute turns on process versus arithmetic: whether changing voting hours so late was unfair in itself, as Bolsonaro said, or whether it made no difference to the outcome, as Nunes Marques said.

How did Lula turn the race around?

More Brazilians voted in the runoff, with abstention falling from 21.08% to 19.22%, according to electoral court figures. Between the rounds, Lula gained 10.38 million votes and Bolsonaro about 3.7 million.

Lula's allies credited a WhatsApp channel called Lula's Army, run by Congressman André Janones, with helping deliver the comeback. The channel grew from about 60,000 followers to nearly 11 million in three weeks. Political scientists said its effect could not be separated from street campaigning and turnout drives.

Lula also campaigned on national sovereignty after the Trump administration imposed 50% tariffs on Brazilian goods over what it called the persecution of Jair Bolsonaro.

What will Lula face?

Analysts including Rachel Ziemba said investors had bet on a Bolsonaro win and tighter control of spending, and expected more spending and higher interest rates under Lula. Public debt already exceeds 82% of Brazil's economic output.

In Congress, Lula will face a center-right and right-wing majority elected on Oct. 4, with Bolsonaro's Liberal Party holding the largest bloc in decades. "Lula has his hands tied," said Guilherme Casarões, an associate professor of Brazilian studies at Florida International University. "Pretty much everything he has tried to do has been blocked in Congress."

As president, Lula will also get to fill four open seats on Brazil's 11-member Supreme Court, where Zanin sits. The New York Times said those choices could shape Brazil's course on divisive issues for years.

Abroad, Lula has kept Brazil out of a U.S.-led military coalition against drug cartels, citing national sovereignty. Will Freeman, a fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, said Lula was pragmatic enough to reach a working arrangement with the Trump administration.

Environmental advocates welcomed the result, citing Lula's pledge to end deforestation by 2030. But they noted continued oil, mining and farming expansion under Lula, and warned the new Congress could roll back forest protections.

What happens next?

Bolsonaro has said he will take legal action. Legal experts said he would face a high bar to have the election annulled.

Analyst Alejo Czerwonko said markets could stay volatile until Lula presents a credible fiscal plan, and that the government would likely keep a gradual approach to stabilizing debt.

Lula is expected to take office on Jan. 5, 2027, for a term running to 2030, with further tariff talks with Washington ahead.
