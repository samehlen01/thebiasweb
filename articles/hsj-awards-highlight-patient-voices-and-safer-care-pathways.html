The 2026 HSJ Patient Safety Awards recognised work across 25 categories, spanning clinical and specialist excellence, organisation-wide change, and service and system innovation. The examples below are projects featured in HSJ’s awards coverage, illustrating efforts to make it easier to raise concerns, address treatment delays and strengthen safety systems. The ceremony was held at Telford International Centre, hosted by Hal Cruttenden, and 135 judges selected winners from 427 entries, 217 of which were shortlisted, after a five-week first stage and around 190 hours of virtual second-stage judging.

Accessible escalation and patient voice

In Bolton, Bolton NHS Foundation Trust identified reliance on verbal escalation routes as an inadvertently created patient-safety risk. Bolton NHS Foundation Trust and Bolton Deaf Society co-designed accessible routes to Martha’s Rule for Deaf community members, making the process for escalating concerns more accessible. Deaf community members helped design, test and implement the solutions. HSJ reported that the work reduced barriers, strengthened patient empowerment and offered a transferable inclusive-safety model.

The Yorkshire and Humber PCC ODN Martha’s Rule pilot used adaptable approaches because paediatric services varied across providers. It prioritised regional engagement with families, children, young people and staff.

The pilot produced principles validated by NHS core standards, a flowchart promoting fresh-eyes reviews, and tools for clinical review. The fresh-eyes reviews offered an additional clinical review, while its workbook addressed advocacy, communication, bias awareness and structured review. That emphasis on co-design and additional review also runs through later examples of care-pathway and service redesign.

Redesigning care

Alastair McLellan argued in the Health Service Journal that patient safety had returned to the top of the health agenda, but public debate had focused overwhelmingly on maternity services. He also highlighted the strong association between emergency-department waits of more than eight hours and increased mortality.

Against that concern about emergency-department waits, HSJ reported that an urgent-care AI project, still early in deployment, uses real-time risk scores to flag people who may be at high risk of returning to an emergency department within a month. Developed across clinical and digital teams, it generates the scores from routine clinical and demographic data. The project is intended to support earlier intervention, targeted community care and multidisciplinary assessment.

PROACT, a community-based Parkinson’s therapy service developed with Parkinson’s UK, addresses harm associated with delayed specialist Parkinson’s therapy, including falls, functional decline and hospital admissions.

HSJ reported that timely home-based interventions reduced waiting times by 90 per cent. HSJ also reported that PROACT reduced healthcare costs and complication risks while improving quality of life.

At Royal Devon University Hospital, a redesigned hyperacute stroke pathway used human-factors principles, including enhanced triage, clearer roles and reduced cognitive load. Its direct-to-CT approach takes patients directly to scanning, while its named Pitstop DIDO model consistently achieved door-to-scan times below 15 minutes. HSJ reported project results including reduced lifelong disability, improved patient experience and savings of about £47,000 ($62,351) in future care needs per patient.

At Dudley Group Foundation Trust, an intensive-care project identified safety risks arising from systems designed primarily for life-prolonging treatment. The ICU became the first UK intensive-care unit to achieve Gold Standards Framework accreditation in 2023. Its palliative-care implementation reported improvements in identifying end-of-life patients, advance care planning and direct discharge to preferred places of care.

In Salford, frontline feedback identified variation in nutrition and hydration practice, including inconsistent Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) screening and care-plan use. HSJ reported significant frailty among Salford residents aged 65 and over, linked to preventable harms including dehydration, malnutrition risk, falls, acute kidney injury (AKI), urinary tract infections (UTIs) and avoidable admissions. Against that backdrop, the Salford Frailty Prevention Partnership rolled out a unified Nutrition and Hydration Safety Programme intended to standardise early identification and consistent person-centred support.

Safety and resources

University Hospitals Birmingham replaced paper-based controlled-drug management with the Alfa platform on 141 wards across four hospital sites. Before its introduction, controlled-drug audit compliance had fallen to 67 per cent against an 85 per cent standard.

100 per cent
ward compliance across all University Hospitals Birmingham sites
All University Hospitals Birmingham sites reached 100 per cent ward compliance by the fourth quarter of 2025/26.

The platform uses real-time discrepancy detection, barcode scanning and electronic signatures compliant with the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001. HSJ reported that Alfa revealed unauthorised transfers and unaccounted losses, while cutting administration time by up to 80 per cent.

University Hospitals Birmingham also developed Listen, Learn, Share as a trust-wide approach to learning from patient-safety events. Developed in response to cultural challenges, it promotes psychological safety and open conversations. More than 200 Learning Ambassadors support the programme across University Hospitals Birmingham sites, and it reported improved staff-survey results across every safety indicator.

Modality Partnership argued that workforce wellbeing is directly linked to patient safety. Its CARE Wellbeing Network established system-level support for psychological safety and proactive wellbeing across an organisation with 55 GP practices and 1,800 staff.

Modality reported a 23.1 per cent reduction in sickness episodes and a 33.4 per cent fall in stress-related absence. It said the model reduced burnout risk, strengthened workforce resilience and supported safer, more reliable care.

In mental-health care, the CNWL NHS Therapeutic Observation and Engagement Collaborative brought together 18 multidisciplinary teams when it launched in November 2024. Its aim was to reduce restrictive interventions and embed therapeutic engagement.

reported fall in violence and aggression incidents
Reported by the collaborative, which launched in November 2024 with 18 multidisciplinary teams.

The collaborative also reported a 37 per cent reduction in observation-related staffing expenditure. The collaborative argued that those results showed safety and financial sustainability were complementary rather than competing priorities in mental-health services.

For the first time, shortlisted organisations were invited to present their projects at the HSJ Patient Safety Congress held alongside the awards, where they shared their work.
