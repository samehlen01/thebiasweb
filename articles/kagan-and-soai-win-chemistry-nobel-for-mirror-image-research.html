The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry jointly to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai for their work on non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis.

12 million Swedish kronor
Shared Nobel Prize amount
Approximately $1.2 million (£0.9 million).

Kagan and Soai are due to receive their awards at a ceremony in Stockholm on December 10.

Life uses particular molecular handedness, while laboratory reactions have commonly produced equal proportions of the two mirror-image forms. The prize-winning research concerns chirality, the property of molecules that exist as mirror-image variants that cannot be superimposed, like right and left hands. Kagan's work showed how a small imbalance between mirror-image forms can be amplified through non-linear effects. Soai's reaction illustrates autocatalysis, in which production of one form reinforces further production of that same form.

Amplifying a molecular imbalance

The researchers' contributions addressed related but distinct mechanisms. Kagan demonstrated that an imbalance between mirror-image forms could be amplified, while Soai developed work on a homochiral autocatalytic reaction.

Soai pursued a homochiral reaction, one that produces only one mirror-image form. In autocatalysis, a chiral product acts as its own catalyst, helping drive further production of the same mirror-image form. Together, these mechanisms create a self-reinforcing asymmetry.

  1. 1986: Kagan showed that chemical reactions could be manipulated to produce a greater excess of one molecular mirror image.
  2. 1995–2003: Soai progressed from describing a reaction with the potential to be homochiral in 1995 to presenting, in 2003, a reaction that produced only one mirror-image form.

The sequence matters because Kagan demonstrated that small imbalances could be amplified, while Soai's reaction provided a homochiral example of autocatalysis.

Peter Somfai calls the Soai reaction an exceptionally important experiment in organic chemistry, crediting it with demonstrating selective, efficient production through autocatalysis. He draws a distinction between the contributions: in Somfai's assessment, Kagan supplied a proof of principle, while Soai supplied the first real experimental example. In that assessment, their combined work reshaped scientific understanding of how chirality is created, amplified and transmitted.

Practical applications

Control over which mirror-image form is produced makes the research relevant to efforts to select a desired molecular form. Different mirror-image versions of a molecule can interact differently with the body and produce different drug effects. For drugmakers, selecting a molecular form can affect drug effects. Heiner Linke says controlling chirality may allow manufacturers to select the desired molecular form and reduce the risk that its mirror image will be ineffective, weaken the intended effect or cause harm. The Royal Swedish Academy credits the discoveries with providing methods and conceptual tools for designing pharmaceutical reactions and supporting the development of modern medicines.

Somfai says the discoveries have become part of catalyst-development practice across industries that need to direct reactions towards a particular mirror-image form. According to Nature, the applications extend beyond pharmaceuticals to materials, perfumes and agricultural chemicals.

Hernandez says the award is a reminder that everyday advances can arise from basic research performed decades earlier. In his view, fundamental research such as the laureates' work deserves support because investment in science now is needed to secure future practical solutions.

Life's molecular handedness

The same mechanisms of amplification and self-reinforcement also bear on questions about life's molecular handedness. Life uses particular molecular handedness, including right-handed DNA and mostly left-handed protein building blocks. Holden Thorp argues that understanding chemistry that selects between mirror images may help explain life's pattern of molecular handedness. But mysteries remain about how chirality first emerged, Soai says, and the field needs to advance further to understand life.

According to Nature, the discoveries may help explain how nature or early life came to overwhelmingly produce one molecular handedness. Nature reported that this interpretation rests on Kagan's demonstration that a small imbalance can be amplified and Soai's finding that a chiral product can reinforce its own formation.

Soai considers the prize recognition an especially exciting personal achievement. Against the remaining uncertainty, Linke describes the discoveries as a spectacular solution to a chemical mystery more than a century old, citing their demonstration of how homochirality can emerge spontaneously.
