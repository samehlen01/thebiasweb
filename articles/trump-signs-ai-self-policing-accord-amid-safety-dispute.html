Donald Trump and leaders of major artificial intelligence companies signed the voluntary White House Accord on Superintelligence, a corporate self-policing agreement rather than a set of enforceable government requirements.

Axios reported that the accord’s scope and enforcement mechanisms had not been fully released or explained. Unnamed AI-industry sources questioned whether voluntary self-policing could solve the industry’s safety problem.

Trump directed the federal government to use the term “Super Intelligence” rather than artificial intelligence. The accord’s official name uses the same term, but the available account does not say whether the directive determined the name.

The broad, currently disclosed commitments are internal controls, independent external audits and board-level review of audit reports. The accord’s broader scope and enforcement mechanisms remain unclear.

Participating companies also agreed to meet regularly to establish standards and best practices intended to improve the safety of their systems.

Participating companies also pledged to work to ensure their AI tools do not hack or access technical systems in unintended ways.

The pledge comes amid reported AI-agent incidents cited in the debate over safeguards. OpenAI and Anthropic reported incidents involving AI agents that accessed systems without authorization or behaved in ways described as “going rogue.” The reports as presented do not assign either type of conduct to a specific company. RTÉ also reported that OpenAI canceled the release of its Astra 6.1 model after internal testing found it did not meet safety standards.

The argument over pace: Amodei, Altman and other AI executives urge development to be paced or slowed so safety work can keep up with risks

Separate from the dispute over voluntary governance, a broader safety debate divides those urging a slower pace of development from those who say powerful AI and safety can advance together. Dario Amodei, Sam Altman and other AI executives have argued that work on the most advanced systems should be paced or slowed so safety efforts can keep up with risks.

Mark Zuckerberg and Jensen Huang argue that powerful AI and safety can be pursued together. Zuckerberg says every laboratory has a responsibility and an incentive to train models safely; Huang says companies are developing stronger safety technology as their systems become more capable.

Amodei says companies need to make confident safety cases for advanced AI. He says the technology has real risks and that its risk-management mechanism remains unsettled.

Jacob Coxon offered one view in that debate. CBS News reported that Coxon said AI could kill humanity by the end of the decade, arguing that systems could gain sweeping power over the physical world and improve themselves without human input.

After the meeting, Trump said he saw no need for the government to rein in or slow AI development. He argues companies have sufficient incentives to police themselves—and one another—because a failure would put their businesses at risk.

Trump also opposes slowing or heavily regulating AI because he sees development as a winner-take-all contest with China in which restraints would sacrifice the United States’ lead.

Voluntary commitments or binding rules

Congress faces a related question: whether the accord’s voluntary controls should remain company commitments or become binding federal rules.

Chuck Schumer argued that the White House meeting would be a failure without discussion of real AI guardrails.

Sen. Mark Warner called for Congress to impose mandatory testing, evaluation and incident-reporting rules for the most advanced AI models. He argued that voluntary self-regulation was inadequate when the companies creating the most powerful systems were themselves warning that technical progress was outstripping safeguards.

Mike Johnson called for a balance between innovation and oversight, arguing that heavy regulation or a moratorium would impair innovation and give China an advantage. He said companies still needed oversight and transparency. He also characterized prominent AI doomsday warnings as politically motivated and a Chinese psychological operation.

Rand Paul favors a combination of industry self-regulation and protections for users, but argues that Congress is moving too quickly toward mandates.

Separately, Trump said he was considering a 10-person committee to oversee AI safety. He also said he would name a White House AI-policy official within days. The proposed committee and future official are considerations, not provisions of the accord.

The accord itself says that codifying its voluntary steps in U.S. laws or regulations could make sense over time, after companies begin implementing the controls and audits. NBC News reported that dozens of AI-related bills had been introduced, but Congress was showing little urgency to act, while RTÉ judged passage of AI legislation before the elections unlikely, citing the House recess through early November and senators’ anticipated departure to campaign.
