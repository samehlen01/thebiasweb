Preliminary assessments indicate that, on 26 August, a collapse involving glacier ice, rock and part of a mountainside generated a debris-laden flood through connected river systems in Nepal and Tibet.

In Nepal, disaster authorities reported 1,114 recovered bodies and 3,916 people missing as of 2 September. The detailed rescue figures that follow concern Nepal.

Recovered bodies reported in Nepal
Nepal disaster authorities’ reported figure as of 2 September

In Tibet, Chinese state media reported 16 deaths and 546 people missing. With thousands still unaccounted for, the final toll remains uncertain.

Nepal’s disaster authorities said more than 21,000 personnel from the police, army and armed police had been deployed, while nearly 12,000 people had been rescued overall. At damaged hydropower projects, 279 people had been rescued and at least 639 remained missing, with some believed trapped in tunnels. Those project-specific figures are subsets of the wider overall rescue and missing-person counts.

How the flood began

The U.S. Geological Survey and scientific investigators have reached the preliminary conclusion that the disaster was caused by a major collapse of glacier ice, rock and part of a mountainside above the Lhende Kola. Rock, ice and meltwater then moved into valleys below as a destructive debris flow through connected river systems in Nepal and Tibet.

The U.S. Geological Survey recorded a magnitude-5.2 seismic signal from the collapse. A surviving water-level gauge at Galchhi, cited by the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development, recorded the Trishuli River rising about 30 feet in half an hour. The readings show the speed of the surge that struck settlements, crossings and industrial facilities downstream.

Scientists Jon Tunnicliffe and Basanta Raj Adhikari warned that debris in river channels and unstable slopes could trigger further landslides, temporary blockages and flood waves. Deposited material may raise riverbeds, reroute water or form short-lived dams as instability continues in slopes and glaciers.

Searches centred on hydropower tunnels

Search teams continue working at damaged hydropower sites and tunnels, where some missing people are believed trapped. The work is complicated by devastated tunnel sites, elapsed days and difficult terrain.

Official Phanindra Paudel and retired army commander Binoj Basnet said the chances of finding survivors were diminishing as time passed, particularly at the most devastated tunnel sites. They cited the elapsed days, the scale of destruction and the condition of the terrain. Declining survival prospects do not establish the fate of those missing.

Official Amhsu Kiran Shahi urged rescuers not to abandon hope, saying some workers may still be alive in protected internal spaces. He said 46 people were thought to be in a room-like structure at Rasuwagadhi that may contain food and water, and that dry areas may remain at other facilities, though teams had not located all possible spaces.

The disaster also damaged infrastructure needed for rescue and recovery. Nepal’s government said at least 48 suspension bridges were damaged, with 28 requiring complete replacement. In the affected area, 11 hydropower stations and one solar plant stopped operating. The loss of crossings restricts movement, while the shutdown of generating sites adds pressure on communities and rescuers.

Displacement, identification and access

The United Nations humanitarian agency said almost 3,500 people were living across 27 displacement sites in Nuwakot and Rasuwa. It reported overcrowded shelters, contaminated water and damaged health facilities. The agency said these conditions raise the risk of disease and extend the emergency beyond searches to shelter, sanitation, water safety and health care.

Nepal Police asked relatives of missing people for DNA samples and photographs to help identify victims as hospitals and morgues ran out of space and unidentified bodies were buried. For families still waiting for news, identification is complicated by the force of the flood and the scale of the recovery effort.

British nationals and an individual rescue-record dispute

The UK Foreign Office said 36 British nationals were unaccounted for in a working count rather than a confirmation of individual outcomes. It said it had sent a rapid-response team to Nepal and committed £5 million ($6.76 million) for relief and recovery. Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal and the Nepal Tourism Board expect the number may fall as trekkers stranded without communications make contact.

The Nepali Army said 119 people, including a British citizen, had been rescued in air operations. But Alastair Chambers, a British aid volunteer, said he had not been rescued and that officials recorded his details after he arrived on a humanitarian helicopter flight, apparently treating his arrival as a rescue. The accounts differ over Chambers’ individual status.

Power, reconstruction and trade

More than 99% of Nepal’s electricity is generated by hydropower, and facilities in the flood zone were buried or damaged. Lost capacity threatens electricity supply.

Nepal’s government estimated reconstruction costs at between $4 billion (£2.96 billion) and $5 billion (£3.7 billion), potentially about one-tenth of the national economy. Saroj Bhandari reported tourism cancellations during what would normally be a peak travel period.

Access-road repairs restored the route to the badly affected Rasuwagadhi/Gyirong crossing, one of Nepal’s principal trading points with China. The link accounts for about one-third of Nepal’s $2.95 billion (£2.18 billion) trade with China. Although road access was restored, trade analyst Posh Raj Pandey said damage to the crossing could still disrupt commerce and increase reliance on other border routes.

Trade analyst Posh Raj Pandey said the disruption showed the risk of concentrating trade and hydropower infrastructure in the same river corridor. He argued that Nepal should develop alternative China trade routes.

Climate risk debate after the collapse

Prime Minister Balendra Shah has presented the flood as evidence of growing climate-related danger in the Himalayas and said responding to such disasters is an international responsibility. He argues that Nepal faces severe consequences from global warming despite contributing comparatively little to the emissions driving it.

Scientists Jon Tunnicliffe and Daniel Shugar said it is too early to attribute this specific glacier failure conclusively to climate change because the precise failure mechanism has not been established. Their caution concerns the unresolved trigger of the 26 August collapse, not the broader changes affecting Himalayan glaciers and permafrost.

Researchers Bethan Davies, Gunjan Silwal and Joseph Shea said warming, glacier retreat and degrading permafrost are likely reducing the stability of Himalayan slopes and glaciers. They cited thinning ice, the loss of glacial support for slopes and thawing ground that had helped bind rock and ice together. Silwal, Shugar and Tunnicliffe expect continued warming to increase the frequency of destabilising ice, rock, landslide and flood hazards.

Kanni Wignaraja, a UN official, warned that broader future Himalayan flood risk could make some riverbank communities effectively uninhabitable as glacial-flood dangers rise. She said technology may not keep pace with losses along major river systems exposed to glacial-lake overflow.

State of play

The immediate priorities are tunnel searches, accounting for missing people, and monitoring unstable slopes and river channels for further hazards. The United Nations humanitarian agency reported almost 3,500 people living across 27 displacement sites in Nuwakot and Rasuwa.
