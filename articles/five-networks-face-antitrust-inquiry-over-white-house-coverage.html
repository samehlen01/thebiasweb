The Justice Department has opened an antitrust investigation into ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News over their suspension of White House television-pool coverage.

How the coverage dispute unfolded

On September 19, the White House barred CNN, MS NOW and Politico from access. RTE reported that reporters from those three outlets had their credentials revoked that day. NPR reported that more than 70 journalists had White House press passes revoked. Trump accused the barred outlets of producing false or unfairly negative reporting.

The White House television pool is a rotating arrangement in which broadcasters cover presidential events and share footage or other material with other outlets. Following the September 19 access restrictions, the five networks agreed not to substitute for CNN on days when the administration blocked CNN from its pool assignment, beginning a four-day suspension of television-pool coverage. During the suspension, some White House events lacked the usual shared network footage for other outlets, including an event between Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. NPR also reported that, at times, other outlets covering the events did not capture what Trump said.

Court-ordered access restoration and remaining travel limits

On September 24, US District Judge Timothy Kelly temporarily ordered the White House to restore access for the affected outlets. He ruled that the ban was likely unconstitutional and later extended the pause on it. On September 25, the networks separately resumed television-pool coverage after the court-ordered restoration of press access. The court-ordered temporary restoration of broader White House access did not end all restrictions on CNN and Politico: They remained unable to board Air Force One and travel with the president. An attorney for the two outlets said that continuing exclusion was harming the companies.

In court, the administration cited national-security concerns, anonymous sourcing, professionalism and alleged failures to meet standards as grounds for the access restrictions. The White House also maintained that covering the president was a privilege rather than a right.

Commercial boycott or protected cooperation?

According to a Justice Department official, the department sent civil investigative demands seeking network communications and materials concerning the pool. The department argues that the broadcasters are commercial competitors whose coordinated boycott of White House events could violate the Sherman Act, warranting an investigation.

The networks, however, said their action protected press access: In a joint statement, they argued that the public has a vital interest in accurate, independent information about government and that administrations should not restrict news organizations because of objections to their reporting. The central dispute is whether the networks’ coordinated conduct should be treated primarily as commercial activity or as cooperation to protect press access.

In a separate supporting opinion, Laura Loomer argued that competing news organizations may have agreed to withhold a product the public expects to see. She said that was the type of concerted action a serious Justice Department antitrust division should examine.

Seth Stern called the inquiry baseless, arguing that defending constitutional press rights was not antitrust conduct and that depriving Trump of attention was not a competitive harm.

William E. Kovacic, in comments reported by Reason, said successful antitrust cases require an unmistakable commercial motive that predominates, and that he did not think that requirement could be demonstrated here.

Michael Dorf pointed to the cooperation that already makes the press pool possible, arguing that limited space at particular venues ordinarily justifies broadcasters working together. If that justifies routine cooperation in coverage, standing up for a free press is at least as important a justification, in his view.

Bruce D. Brown challenged the legitimacy of the inquiry, arguing that decisions about news coverage are protected by the First Amendment. He said the investigation appeared to be a pretext to obtain networks’ coverage records and intimidate journalists, following the administration’s earlier moves to bar outlets. Separately, Jose Zamora said the government should not use antitrust investigations to intimidate outlets defending press access, arguing that the public’s right to know depends on that access.
