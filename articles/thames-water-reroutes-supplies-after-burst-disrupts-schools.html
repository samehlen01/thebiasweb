Thames Water reroutes supplies after burst disrupts schools

Thames Water said customers should have access to water again by the day after a burst main in West Molesey disrupted supplies across Surrey and south London. The company rerouted supplies, but warned that a permanent repair could take days.

The company said up to 15,000 properties were initially affected, before the reported number fell to about 6,000 with either no water or low pressure. The disruption forced schools and colleges to close or send pupils home, while some businesses shut and lost money.

The later estimate covered five south London boroughs and two Surrey boroughs, according to Thames Water. Its list of 18 potentially affected postcodes included locations in the KT, SW and TW areas, alongside CR4 and SM4.

The burst was reported near Island Barn Reservoir, although Thames Water said the reservoir itself was not the source of the leak. Monica Harding said it was her understanding that water was escaping at 750 litres a second early on Monday morning after a large main burst.

Thames Water said specialist teams attended the site to stop flooding from the broken pipe and later isolated the main. Alongside the company's work to reroute supplies through its network, tankers were used to support the mains, Surrey Live reported.

Closures and emergency water

At Hollyfield School and Sixth Form Centre, the loss of supply raised a safety concern. The school said its available water was expected to run out and students could not safely remain once that happened.

For affected businesses, the interruption meant closures and financial losses. Many were able to resume trading after water was rerouted, the Evening Standard reported, allowing them to reopen without waiting for the permanent repair to the damaged main.

Some shops ran out of bottled water during the disruption, according to Surrey Live. Thames Water said its bottled-water response included deliveries and collection stations, with customers on its Priority Services Register given priority. Bottled-water provision remained available the following day.

Harding called the outage unacceptable and urged Thames Water to respond immediately. Her demand was both to restore supply to residents who had lost water and to protect vulnerable people during the disruption.

Infrastructure and ownership under criticism

On the immediate cause, the Guardian reported accounts suggesting that a contractor may have struck the water pipe with a digger, causing a four-foot rupture in the Walton section.

Harding blamed the incident on wider failures of infrastructure and regulation, arguing that years of inadequate investment and poor regulation had contributed to failures. She distinguished that criticism from the work of the repair teams, praising their efforts while saying they were dealing with the consequences of failed infrastructure.

Luke Taylor said Thames Water was no longer fit for purpose, citing its daily leakage and the burst's effects on carers, businesses and schools. His criticism extended beyond the immediate task of getting water flowing again.

Ed Davey argued that Thames Water should be replaced because it was failing customers. He pointed to leakage and the share of bills used to service debt rather than repair infrastructure as grounds for that judgment.

Sophie Conquest called for public ownership. In comments reported by the Evening Standard, she framed the choice as serving hedge funds or serving households, arguing that public ownership would allow the service to operate in the interests of households, workers and the environment.

Thames Water cited the location of the burst when warning that repair work was likely to take time. It said three small trees had been removed so crews could reach the pipe safely. Materials needed for the permanent fix were due later in the week, the company said.
