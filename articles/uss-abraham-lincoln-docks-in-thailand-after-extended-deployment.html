The event known as USS Abraham Lincoln docks in Thailand after extended deployment began on Wednesday, when the US aircraft carrier arrived at Laem Chabang, near Pattaya. The Navy described the visit as the ship’s first full port stop since its November deployment began. About 5,000 sailors and Marines were associated with the call, which was intended to provide a four-day period of rest and recreation before the carrier’s return journey to the United States.

286 days
At sea before the first full port stop
The US Navy’s measure of the Lincoln’s deployment since November.

The duration has been reported using different measures rather than as a clear contradiction. The Navy put the period since deployment began at 286 days, while the Guardian described the crew as having spent more than 270 days at sea. The BBC and Forbes used a different yardstick, reporting more than 250 days without a port call. The ship had left San Diego for what was expected to be a Pacific and South China Sea assignment, but was rerouted to the Middle East after US-Israeli military operations against Iran began. The USS George Washington later relieved it there.

A US military official said each service member would receive three liberty days and one day of duty aboard the ship during the stop. Thai officials said the Lincoln would then head back to the United States. Rear Adm. Robert E. Loughran presented the break as deserved recovery for sailors and Marines after the extended mission, and as a demonstration of the US-Thai alliance. Kristopher Robinson, the US naval attaché, said Pattaya had been selected for rest and relaxation because Thailand had a history of welcoming American sailors and other visitors.

A tightly managed port call

Thai authorities paired the welcome with unusually visible controls. Pattaya Mayor Poramase Ngampiches imposed restrictions that included bans on cannabis use and water activities such as parasailing and jet-skiing. He also said policing, transport arrangements and fair-pricing measures were meant to protect visitors and prevent exploitation. Provincial Governor Narit Niramaiwong said officials would provide security and other assistance during the visit.

About 600 officials will provide security and other necessary assistance,
— Narit Niramaiwong, provincial governor

The governor’s figure illustrated the scale of the local preparations. Pattaya police chief Anek Srathongyoo said any US service member found to have broken Thai law would be prosecuted, a position that also defines the expected consequence for violations. Police had arrested 25 women for soliciting on the beachfront promenade a week before the carrier arrived. Ngampiches acknowledged the crackdown but maintained that it was not connected to the Lincoln’s visit. Separately, the Independent speculated that personnel were likely reminded that prostitution is technically illegal in Thailand and that paying for sex is punishable under US military law; that reported reminder has not been independently confirmed.

The mayor nevertheless welcomed the prospect of further US troop visits, whether for exercises or holidays. He also argued that Pattaya should not be reduced to its sex-tourism reputation, saying the city could promote sports, wellness, seminars and family-oriented activities instead. His argument placed the naval stop within a wider effort to present Pattaya as a more varied destination, even as the authorities’ restrictions and enforcement measures showed awareness of the city’s longstanding image.

100 million baht
Potential local spending from the sailors’ visit
($3.01 million / £2.22 million) Pattaya officials forecast as much as about $3 million (£2.22 million), not a confirmed outcome.

Local business figures shared the expectation of a commercial boost. Anthony “AJ” Jones described the visit as the talk of the town and expected it to benefit businesses, while merchant Anon Saiteer said he hoped sailors would spend money because local firms had little to lose. Those views align with Pattaya officials’ forecast of substantial spending, but the projected economic injection remains a forecast rather than an established result of the port call.

Crew welfare remains disputed

The break in Thailand has also focused attention on unresolved reports about life aboard the Lincoln. Multiple news accounts alleged food shortages, faulty plumbing or toilets, poor living conditions and mental-health problems during the deployment. Captain Dan Keeler said the ship had enough food overall, while acknowledging that fresh fruit and vegetables had become difficult to obtain and were no longer served. He argued that US reporting about the vessel was disconnected from the reality aboard it. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth went further, calling the reports of conditions completely misrepresented.

An unnamed Lincoln sailor’s wife offered the opposing account, alleging that her husband considered the mission his worst deployment and that both the quality and quantity of food had been unacceptable because portions were rationed. Rep. Mike Levin said the planned relief was insufficient and called on the Pentagon to provide clear information about the reported conditions and the operational failures he said must be examined to avoid repetition. The claims from the sailor’s family and Levin’s demand for accountability therefore remain at odds with Keeler’s and Hegseth’s rejection of the reporting.

Julie Roland, commenting on the allegations, argued that lengthy naval missions should not leave sailors dealing with water contamination or lacking basic care. She pointed to burdens including limited sunlight below deck, crowded triple bunks and postponed port calls. Her analysis echoed a Military Medicine study that associated poor habitability, noise and demanding schedules with inadequate sleep and serious psychological distress. Sen. Richard Blumenthal said repeated extensions of recent carrier deployments could indicate that prolonged missions are becoming routine in the Navy’s force-generation model. Donald Trump expressed the sharply different view that the deployment had not been nearly long enough.

State of play

What is agreed is that the Lincoln reached Laem Chabang after an exceptionally long deployment, with Thailand providing a controlled period of leave before the ship returns to the United States. Thai authorities have confirmed security arrangements, activity restrictions and the prospect of prosecution for breaches of local law, while officials and businesses expect the visit to bring economic activity. Allegations about food, sanitation, living conditions and mental health aboard the carrier remain disputed: Keeler and Hegseth contest the portrayal, while a sailor’s wife, Levin and Roland urge a more critical examination. The next reported step is the Lincoln’s journey home after the Thailand call.
