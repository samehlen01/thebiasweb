Anak Krakatau, in Indonesia’s Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra, sent ash over Jakarta, Banten, West Java, Lampung and Bengkulu, prompting AirNav Indonesia to restrict airspace around Jakarta airports and other affected airports. By Monday, the transport ministry reported cumulative operational effects since 5 September 2026 involving more than 270,000 passenger impacts, described in the ministry’s tally as stranded passengers, and more than 2,000 affected flights.

Eruption, ash and safety restrictions

After showing signs of unrest since Friday, Anak Krakatau entered a continuous eruptive phase at 11:07pm Friday that lasted about 25 hours and ended at 12:04am Sunday. After the phase ended, the Geological Agency reported a 30-second eruption at 3:53am Sunday and separately recorded a 16-second eruption about three hours later.

Indonesia’s volcanology agency and the Darwin Volcanic Ash Advisory Centre identified two major ash clouds. The cloud on the Java side moved northeast over Jakarta, Banten, West Java and nearby waters. The cloud on the Sumatra side spread over parts of Banten, Lampung and Bengkulu.

Anak Krakatau remained at Level III, or Alert, and authorities maintained a three-kilometre exclusion zone around the volcano. They reported no casualties or evacuation order. Officials issued no tsunami warning and said there was no current tsunami potential. Local port authorities warned that volcanic activity may disrupt navigation through the Sunda Strait and told vessels to remain vigilant and stay outside the three-kilometre exclusion zone.

The Geological Agency said further eruptions remain likely despite the end of the continuous phase. The agency said repeated eruptions indicated a continuing supply of magma and volcanic gases beneath the volcano, but officials could not predict whether the unrest would lead to a larger eruption. It warned that, if activity resumes or continues, incandescent rocks, heavy ashfall and lava flows could become hazards.

A rapidly expanding aviation disruption

The Ministry of Transportation’s early Sunday assessment put the impact at 516 flight movements, including 202 at Jakarta’s Soekarno-Hatta airport, affecting about 22,800 passengers. A separate report citing the ministry described more than 22,000 affected passengers and 209 flights, particularly services to and from Singapore. Because the reports use different units and do not establish matching airports, periods or types of disruption, they are separate, non-comparable tallies.

To date, 1,558 flights have been delayed at the eight affected airports, with 170,000 passengers stranded
— Indonesia’s Ministry of Transportation

In a Sunday update, the ministry said 963 of the 1,558 flights were at Soekarno-Hatta. Separate reports described 1,558 flights as cancelled, delayed or both and referred to an eight-airport disruption and a 170,000-passenger total, but did not establish that their scope or classifications matched the ministry tally.

The ministry said its more than 270,000 figure was a cumulative measure of passenger impacts since 5 September 2026, including cancellations, delays and diversions, rather than a one-day count. It described the passengers as stranded but did not state whether the total represented unique people or passenger journeys affected by the different forms of disruption.

Airlines altered schedules as restrictions spread. Singapore Airlines cancelled nine Singapore-Jakarta flights scheduled for Monday, while Scoot and Qantas also reported disrupted Jakarta services. Qantas said cancellations had affected routes to and from the Indonesian capital, and the Directorate General of Civil Aviation reported disruption at Bali’s I Gusti Ngurah Rai International Airport and at Medan. The UK government advised British travellers to check with their airline or travel provider for current flight-status information.

Lukman F. Laisa, the civil-aviation chief, said aviation safety must take precedence in every operational decision during the eruption. Volcanic ash can damage jet engines, turbine blades, fuel and air systems, and windscreens. Laisa said restoring services could put pressure on aircraft rotations, fleet availability and terminal capacity even after airports reopen.

Travel disruption and ash precautions

Travellers described sharply different experiences of the disruption. Michele Gouw described the eruption as an unavoidable disruption caused by a natural disaster and said passengers had little choice but to accept the inconvenience and await airline and airport instructions. Michel Miegge and Carol Odile described poor or absent airline information, uncertainty over accommodation and alternative flights, and disappointment over cancelled plans. Jo Shaw said an airport-hotel delay was a comparatively minor inconvenience, but she was more concerned about conservation teams working with rhinos and the rhinos facing the environmental conditions.

Health authorities warned that ash exposure may worsen air quality and urged particular care for people with asthma or other respiratory conditions. The health minister urged residents to stay indoors and wear face masks. Indonesian authorities allowed ash-affected schools to move temporarily to online learning, while school activities and fishing trips were also interrupted.

Restrictions continue as officials monitor conditions

Airport restrictions continued into Monday. Angkasa Pura said seven airports, including Soekarno-Hatta, had extended their closures until 6pm local time. In a separate update, Angkasa Pura said at least four, including Soekarno-Hatta, had extended their closures until 6pm local time; the four-airport figure was a minimum count, not a total of all closures. Transport Minister Dudy Purwagandhi said authorities could not determine when eruption-related restrictions would end because weather conditions remained dynamic.

Indonesia’s National Disaster Mitigation Agency, BNPB, raised the possibility of weather-modification work if conditions permit. Its proposal includes cloud-seeding to encourage heavy rain that the agency believes could wash ash from areas where it is disrupting daily life. The measure remains a potential response rather than a confirmed solution.
