Christa Pike remained critically ill, unconscious, intubated and on a ventilator at a Nashville-area hospital after Wednesday's execution attempt, her attorneys said. The cause of Pike's survival remains under investigation.

Multiple witnesses said they could hear Pike breathing and snoring loudly after the drugs were administered, including after the curtains to the execution chamber were closed. On Friday, her legal representatives filed an emergency state-court motion seeking to preserve evidence from the execution attempt.

Colleen Slemmer's mother, May Martinez, said she had attended expecting the execution to be carried out. Pike's survival, she said, denied justice to her daughter and family.

Calling the outcome tragic, Governor Bill Lee said Tennesseans deserve answers. He ordered an independent third-party review and halted Tennessee's remaining scheduled executions for the year as the cause of Pike's survival remains under investigation.

Pike's legal representatives, Stephen Ferrell and Luke Ihnen, allege that improperly placed or failed IV lines caused pentobarbital to enter tissue rather than her bloodstream. They say the execution team failed to recognize the problem.

That account conflicts with the Department of Correction's statement that it followed every step of the state's established execution protocol and that the protocol did not authorize additional procedures beyond those already carried out during the attempt. The independent review has not determined the cause of Pike's survival.

Tennessee's lethal-injection process calls for two intravenous lines to be placed. If the first IV site fails, the execution team can switch to the second IV site. Tennessee allows a second 100 mg dose of pentobarbital if the condemned person is still alive five minutes after the first dose. During Wednesday's execution attempt, Tennessee administered two doses of pentobarbital, but Pike remained alive. The review has not established how those provisions operated in Pike's case.

Randy Spivey, Pike's post-conviction counsel, reported that at least seven needles were used while the execution team tried to establish IV access. Ferrell and Ihnen said both of Pike's arms were swollen, burned and blistered when she arrived at the hospital.

In broader historical context, the Death Penalty Information Center described Pike's survival after receiving execution drugs as unprecedented in the modern death-penalty era. Earlier unsuccessful attempts, it said, often ended before drugs could be administered because execution teams could not establish IV access.

The Ed Stanton review found that Tennessee had not complied with its revised lethal-injection process since 2018 and that its unidentified executioner had no formal healthcare training. That finding concerned the earlier process in place from 2018; Tennessee switched to a one-drug pentobarbital execution protocol in 2024.

With the cause of Pike's survival still unresolved, Tennessee Sen. Joey Hensley said Tennessee needs better-trained execution personnel, particularly when IV access is difficult. He said he doubted staff were adequately trained to handle difficult patients and speculated that Pike's IV line had been improperly inserted.

Healthcare workers in Tennessee have called for a death-penalty moratorium, an overhaul of execution methods and the removal of medical professionals from executions. Their position is an ethical objection to medical participation: they argue that it conflicts with the duty to preserve life and the American Medical Association's ethics rules, and that, in failed IV-access cases, problems are predictable when participants disregard professional ethics.

In a separate political response, Marsha Blackburn has called for Tennessee to replace lethal injection with the electric chair.

Joel Zivot, an anesthesiologist advising Pike's defense team, said she may have suffered a brain injury because of a delay before resuscitation.

With the cause of Pike's survival still unresolved, legal scholar Eric Berger said her survival does not necessarily prevent Tennessee from seeking another execution date. He cited Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber as the principal precedent, saying it does not categorically forbid a second attempt after an unsuccessful execution attempt. Her medical condition and the investigation's findings could nevertheless complicate another attempt, he said.

If Pike has suffered brain damage, Berger said, a neurological injury could be raised in an argument that she is incompetent to be executed under what he described as the constitutional rule barring execution of severely mentally incompetent people.

Spivey, Pike's post-conviction counsel, has called for her sentence to be commuted, arguing that Tennessee is incapable of carrying out executions consistently with basic human dignity. Pike's legal representatives characterize the execution attempt as cruel and torturous, citing the repeated IV attempts, severe pain, burns and continued breathing after two doses. They argue that those circumstances did not meet constitutional standards.
