On Friday, the Parades Commission issued its determination after 2pm. Justice Patricia Smyth then granted a temporary injunction against an Orange Order procession on Garvaghy Road in Portadown. The Court of Appeal overturned the injunction. In a separate, later High Court challenge to the commission’s determination, Justice Michael Humphreys dismissed the challenge.

The Parades Commission’s restricted authorisation remained in force. Rather than the 60 participants requested for a 1pm start, it authorised a maximum of 35 people to depart no later than 8am. The authorised procession was to be silent, with no bands or accompanying supporters.

Large groups of protesters blocked Garvaghy Road on Sunday morning, delaying the scheduled 8am start of the authorised procession.

Judicial sequence

On Friday, the Parades Commission issued its determination after 2pm. Justice Smyth then granted a temporary injunction halting the march, which the Court of Appeal later overturned. Justice Smyth warned that the longstanding dispute could spread and intensify elsewhere in Northern Ireland. Lady Chief Justice Siobhan Keegan said the timing of the commission’s decision had created a major practical problem and must not be repeated.

In the separate High Court challenge to the commission’s determination, Denise Kiley KC argued that Police Service of Northern Ireland material supplied to the commission concentrated on past risks and did not adequately assess future danger. Tony McGleenan KC said it was unprincipled to treat the notified Orange Order procession as unnotified. He maintained that lawful assembly is presumed and argued that commission determinations should not repeatedly return to court.

Humphreys’ final hearing began at 12.20am on Sunday and ended at 2.15am. He dismissed the challenge to the commission’s determination, saying it had considered evidence from relevant bodies, weighed the central issues and reached a reasoned decision. He said a court could not replace the commission’s judgement with its own and could intervene only where no reasonable decision-maker could have reached the result.

Protest, policing and safety concerns

Organisers initially estimated that a proposed countermarch could draw up to 5,000 people before revising the projected attendance to 300.

The Police Service of Northern Ireland warned protesters that participating in the proposed countermarch, referred to as an unnotified parade, could result in arrest. It mounted a significant security operation with additional officers brought in to support the operation. Jon Boutcher said cooperation and compliance with the law were needed to preserve a peaceful Northern Ireland.

Fr Peter Clarke expressed concern that the early procession would coincide with Catholic parishioners travelling to and from Mass.

Political and community reactions

Michelle O’Neill called the appeal outcome and the authorised march disgraceful. She argued that the march was bound up with a history of hate, sectarian violence and murder, and said unionist leaders had championed it. Mary Lou McDonald called the treatment of the Garvaghy Road community intolerable and urged the Secretary of State and the PSNI chief constable to stop the procession. She said residents did not want to be pulled back into the past. Claire Hanna called the parade proposal provocative and said bad-faith actors appeared intent on reopening old hurt and trauma.

The Irish Government warned of a return to past divisions despite reconciliation progress under the Good Friday Agreement. Gavin Robinson said political leaders should uphold lawful decisions rather than heighten opposition to them. He argued that the rule of law could not operate only when Sinn Féin approved of an outcome and said Northern Ireland’s institutions depend on respect for the police, courts and law.

Carla Lockhart said nationalist residents had nothing to fear from the march, condemned violence and threats, and urged unionists to pursue the matter through legal means. Emma McIlveen argued that stopping the march would create a hecklers’ veto and weaken minority rights. She said the Orange Order had made extensive efforts to engage the local community, but argued that the dispute risked rewarding those who shouted loudest. The Parades Commission said its determination should not be regarded as a victory for one side or a loss for the other.
