The Parades Commission had allowed the Orange Order march along Garvaghy Road with restrictions, but the procession did not take place because protesters blocked the route. Bryant said he had spent the previous fortnight trying to secure a mutually acceptable resolution between the Orange Order and Garvaghy Road residents, but the talks failed. PSNI Chief Constable Jon Boutcher then requested a review of the determination under the Public Processions Act. Northern Ireland Secretary Sir Chris Bryant subsequently banned the Drumcree Orange Order procession, replacing the Parades Commission’s earlier restricted determination.

Stand-offs in the mid-to-late 1990s were accompanied by riots, roadblocks and killings. Against that historical backdrop, Bryant and police said allowing the procession to proceed would create an unacceptable risk of serious injury, disorder and wider unrest across Northern Ireland. Bryant cited changed circumstances in Portadown and the difficulty of safely allowing the procession to proceed, saying the ban served peace and the public interest. Bryant said maintaining the stand-off also carried risks, including protests spreading across Northern Ireland and a further deterioration in community relations.

Safety and legal challenge

Boutcher warned that forcing the march through by a major public-order intervention would have catastrophic long-term effects on confidence in policing, the Executive and the peace process.

Boutcher said the operation was imposing costs and operational pressures that the force could not sustain indefinitely.

The Orange Order’s lawyers are challenging Bryant’s decision under the Public Processions Act as unlawful and irrational. Separately, the Parades Commission’s view was that its original determination did not now have enduring legal effect in light of the chief constable’s current assessment.

Political reactions and investigation

Political reactions included Jim Allister’s argument that police could have cleared the road and escorted the 35 marchers. He accused Boutcher and Bryant of “surrendering to a mob” and allowing “mob rule” to defeat the rule of law. He said the episode taught people that resorting to violence was how to prevail.

Gavin Robinson described Bryant’s intervention as “disgraceful appeasement”, arguing that it subordinated the rule of law to threats of violence and disorder. He argued that Bryant had acted after republican mobilisation and said the exceptional powers had not been invoked even during some of the worst periods of the Troubles. Robinson also questioned whether there had been a dissident-republican threat or intelligence that firearms had been brought to the area.

Claire Hanna said the parade should never have been permitted and should not be allowed again, arguing that permission had caused unnecessary tension, disruption to residents, policing pressure and damage to community relations. Sinn Féin leaders Mary Lou McDonald and Michelle O’Neill welcomed the ban as relief for Garvaghy Road residents. They said an old wound had been reopened, causing fear and anxiety, and that the previous period of peace should have been protected.

Police have begun a large-scale criminal investigation into potential offences during the 12-day stand-off. The PSNI said it had begun sending interview requests to people reasonably suspected of committing offences linked to the protests.

Police invited Michelle O’Neill and other politicians to voluntary interviews.

Responding to the investigation, John O’Dowd said residents had protested peacefully against an attempt to impose an unwanted march by people opposed to reconciliation. He said he supported the residents because he regarded their cause as a defence of the Good Friday Agreement and its institutions, and called for reconciliation rather than demonising or criminalising the community.

The interview invitations prompted a political dispute involving Carla Lockhart. After Michelle O’Neill received her invitation, Lockhart said there could be no business as usual politically. She called for the DUP to use its powers to have O’Neill dealt with appropriately over the Garvaghy Road protests.

Robinson said the DUP should not collapse the Stormont Executive in response to the ban. He questioned what bringing down the Executive would achieve for the Orangemen or the wider situation, and said he would not want to hand authority to Bryant.

In a procedural ruling on the challenge, Lady Justice McBride considered the case important and accepted that it had some urgency, but found that it was not an emergency case. The challenge was adjourned until Monday.
