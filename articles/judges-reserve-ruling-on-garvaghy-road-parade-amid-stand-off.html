The Parades Commission authorised a restricted Orange Order parade of 35 participants along the Garvaghy Road at 8am. The commission’s decision authorising the procession is known as the determination. On 27 September, the High Court refused the residents leave to seek judicial review of that determination. The residents appealed. After hearing the residents’ challenge, the Court of Appeal reserved judgment. Lady Chief Justice Dame Siobhan Keegan said judges would work swiftly but could not give a date for their decision. The appeal also includes a dispute over whether the determination remains valid.

The permitted procession has not gone ahead because police have prevented it amid concerns about disorder and the ongoing protest.

The historic Drumcree parade down the Garvaghy Road has been banned since 1998. That ban concerns the historic parade, not the current restricted procession authorised by the commission. The late-1990s stand-off led to violent clashes for several summers and political tension requiring major security operations.

Appeal arguments and status of the determination

At the appeal, the dispute centred on the commission’s handling of disorder risk. Denise Kiley KC said police had no specific intelligence of planned disorder but had nonetheless described an uncertain risk picture, preparing for outcomes from peaceful passage to violence. She argued that the commission wrongly treated the absence of specific intelligence as answering that broader risk.

Responding to Kiley’s allegation of legal error, the commission’s counsel, Tony McGleenan KC, said the commission made no legal error. He said it had received police intelligence briefings and understood both the uncertainty and police capacity. McGleenan argued that its 31-paragraph analysis should be assessed as a whole, rather than through isolated phrases, and that courts should defer to the expert statutory body.

McGleenan also argued that memories of the 1990s dispute concerned something categorically different from the present proposal. He said the restricted parade differs from earlier applications in its numbers, timing and symbols or emblems.

Separate from the dispute over disorder risk, residents’ solicitor Darragh Mackin says the time-limited determination may have expired. If it has, he argues, a court ruling would have no practical effect because there would be no authorised parade for police to manage. Mackin said a fresh application would be required once a determination lapses.

Positions on the parade and policing

Portadown District Orange Order maintains it has an established legal right to complete the authorised march. It says the state must facilitate that right, that abandoning the parade would legitimise what it calls mob rule, and that officials must take responsibility for failing to enable it. The lodge said it remained open to dialogue for a lasting resolution, provided the law was upheld and that right was given effect.

Residents’ representative Breandán Mac Cionnaith opposes a march that residents regard as sectarian, triumphalist and unacceptable. Residents’ representatives say the community has not authorised a compromise and that no march is preferable to any partial arrangement.

Failed talks, competing responses

Talks led by Northern Ireland Secretary Chris Bryant between Orange Order representatives and Garvaghy Road residents ended without agreement. The negotiations discussed mediation, an arrangement under which the right to march would be recognised but voluntarily not used, and a final march followed by no further applications.

Calling for direct intervention, Mary Lou McDonald says Bryant has failed to intervene during an 11-day impasse and has not engaged sufficiently with residents. She argues that he has the powers to stop the march and should use them to end a dangerous limbo whose consequences residents are bearing, enabling repair in Garvaghy Road and beyond.

Offering withdrawal of the march as a different response, Gerry Carroll says Portadown District could end the dispute by withdrawing it. He argues that court delays worsen residents’ anxiety, credits community mobilisation with protecting residents, and predicts opposition will continue whatever the judges decide.

By contrast, Micheál Martin argues that one intervention will not resolve the conflict. He advocates mediation and says talks could produce an outcome if dialogue continues.

Helen McEntee says Northern Ireland’s history shows lasting solutions require both sides to be able to move forward, making agreement between the Order and residents necessary regardless of the appeal ruling. Residents have called for outside mediation, and McEntee has said she believes an international mediator is likely to be appointed.

Political fallout

Unionist parties have backed no-confidence or exclusion motions against Michelle O’Neill and three other Sinn Féin ministers over their participation in the protest. Timothy Gaston argues that joining an unnotified protest that prevented a permitted parade breached the Pledge of Office and the duty to uphold the rule of law.

In response to the criticism and the motions, O’Neill says she is proud to stand with residents facing an unwanted march. She argues that doing what is right can mean opposing a law she regards as wrong, while maintaining that she remains committed to the Good Friday Agreement and her ministerial obligations.

BBC News NI reported that the effort to remove the ministers lacks the necessary cross-community majority.
