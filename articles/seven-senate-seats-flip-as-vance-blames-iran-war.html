Democrats won control of both houses of Congress in Tuesday's U.S. midterm elections, flipping seven Republican-held Senate seats in a rout that Vice President JD Vance blamed on President Donald Trump's war with Iran.

Republicans had held the House 218-214 and the Senate 53-47, so Democrats needed net gains of four seats in each chamber. CBS News estimates Democrats will hold 251 to 268 of the 435 House seats. The party has secured at least 54 Senate seats, with races in Mississippi and Alaska still being counted.

From next year, the new majorities give Democrats control of congressional committees, the power to subpoena officials, more say over spending and the ability to block Trump's nominees. But Trump's veto and the Senate's filibuster rules mean most bills will still need Republican support to become law.

Trump said he was “absolutely willing to accept” the results and would work with Democrats.

Hakeem Jeffries of New York, the House Democratic leader, has launched a bid for speaker and is expected to become the first Black person in the job. “The days of the House of Representatives functioning like a wholly owned subsidiary of the Trump administration are over,” Jeffries said.

House Speaker Mike Johnson told aides he would not seek to lead his party's House members in the minority.

Where did Democrats win?

The Senate pickups came in Texas, Kansas, South Carolina, Ohio, Iowa, North Carolina and Maine. In Texas, Democrat James Talarico defeated Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton, becoming the first Democrat to win a U.S. Senate election there since 1988. Adam Hamilton became Kansas's first elected Democratic senator since 1932.

In the House, Democrats won conservative-leaning seats from Indiana to Missouri to Alabama, while Republican efforts to redraw district maps in Texas and Florida gained them just one seat.

The gains extended beyond Washington. Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated Republican Gov. Greg Abbott and will become Texas's first Latina governor. Democrats also took the Texas House, though Republicans kept the state Senate.

Republicans held on in Florida, where Sen. Ashley Moody beat Democrat Angie Nixon and Byron Donalds was elected the state's first Black governor.

Why did Republicans lose?

The debate among Republicans turns on whether voters punished Trump's own decisions, above all the war, or weak candidates and the party in Washington.

“Everyone knows why we lost,” Vance wrote on social media. He said turnout was not the problem, since many people who voted for Trump and Vance in 2024 backed Democrats this time, and that voters wanted the war ended and more focus on inflation.

Trump launched the war, and exit polls showed most voters thought it was the wrong decision, including roughly one in four Republicans.

An NBC News exit poll of more than 79,000 voters found that 75% rated the economy negatively and 65% said Trump's policies had made it worse. Some 43% said they cast their House vote to oppose Trump, against 16% who voted to support him.

Trump partly agreed with Vance. “Well, I think it's largely true because it drove the gasoline up a little bit short term,” he said. But he said he would do it again: “You can't let Iran have a nuclear weapon.”

Trump rejected the idea that voters had repudiated him, blaming weak Republican candidates and saying the enthusiasm at his rallies could not be transferred to them.

Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis said Washington Republicans should have voted sooner to release the Jeffrey Epstein files and banned congressional stock trading. He called the result “close to an extinction-level event for Republicans in many parts of the country as we have seen probably in the last 100 years.”

What will Democrats do with power?

Democratic leaders said lowering costs would guide their majorities, including work on energy bills, health insurance subsidies and Medicaid. Jeffries has promised to try to reverse the Medicaid cuts Republicans in Congress approved in 2025.

Some smaller measures already have support in both parties, such as a proposed $35 (£25.98) cap on insulin costs in private insurance plans.

On Iran, Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said: “This war must end. It must end.” He said Congress's power over funding could deter a president who wages war without public consent.

Democrats have also pushed war-powers resolutions, which would force Trump to withdraw troops from Iran. Chatham House, a policy institute, said more pressure would not guarantee a change in policy, because the president keeps wide foreign-policy powers.

Schumer also backed investigations and court action whenever Trump defies the law or the public's will. Democrat Robert Garcia, who will chair the House Oversight Committee, said inquiries would cover Trump family business dealings, government contracts, homeland security and immigration enforcement, and the Epstein files.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis dismissed prospective investigations as shams and Democratic policies as radical, saying both would work against Americans.

Hamilton, the Kansas senator-elect, said Democrats should focus on helping Americans and bipartisan work rather than spending the next two years chiefly on investigations or impeachment.

Progressive House Democrats Delia Ramirez and Greg Casar urged bold, rapid action, while centrist Josh Gottheimer called the result a win for the middle ground.

What happens next?

House Republicans are scheduled to choose Johnson's successor on November 10. Reps. Jim Jordan of Ohio, who wants a combative approach to the Democratic majority, and Steve Scalise of Louisiana, who has urged more unity, are running.

In Mississippi, roughly 1,100 votes separated Democrat Scott Colom and Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith on Wednesday evening. State law has no automatic recount for statewide races, and Colom said his side was prepared to go to court.

In Alaska, where Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan faces Democrat Mary Peltola, overseas ballots can arrive up to 15 days after the election. If no candidate wins more than half of first-choice votes, voters' backup choices will come into play.

Neither race will change which party controls the Senate.
