Trump Administration Lowers Fuel-Economy Standards, Promising Cheaper New Cars

The Trump administration has finalized weaker federal fuel-economy standards for new vehicles, setting an industry fleetwide average of about 34.9 miles per gallon for model year 2031, rather than the roughly 50.4 mpg the Biden-era standard would have required for that model year, according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.

What the rule changes

NHTSA outlined the main changes in the revised standards:

  • NHTSA says the stated model-year range is 2022 through 2031 for passenger cars and light trucks, and the rules require carmakers to make their new passenger-car and truck fleets up to 1% more fuel efficient each year.
  • NHTSA says the rules end automakers’ ability to trade credits for electric-vehicle production and exclude plug-in vehicles from an automaker’s fleet average.

The administration and auto-industry supporters, including the Alliance for Automotive Innovation, argue that the previous standards effectively required a switch to electric vehicles. They say the requirements were out of step with consumer demand and market conditions, limiting manufacturers’ freedom to build the vehicles consumers want.

Purchase prices and fuel bills

According to the Department of Transportation, the revised standards would reduce the average price of a new vehicle in 2031 by about $1,300 (£979.94). It separately projects $138 billion (£104 billion) in consumer savings over five years. The department also expects the rollback to increase gasoline consumption and add about $1,600 (£1,206) in lifetime fuel costs per vehicle. As presented, the projections have different scopes and time horizons and do not by themselves establish a net consumer cost or show whether the $138 billion (£104 billion) figure includes added fuel expense.

The administration’s economic case is that fuel-efficiency technology adds to vehicle costs, while lower production costs would allow more consumers to buy newer cars. The Transportation Department expects the changes to encourage auto sales and U.S. manufacturing.

Analysts including Sue Helper, Mark Jacobsen and James Michael Sallee question whether weaker standards will deliver the promised affordability gains. They argue that manufacturers are not required to pass lower compliance costs on to buyers, so cheaper production does not necessarily mean a lower retail price. Other factors discussed in the analysis include larger vehicles, tariffs, supply-chain problems and added features, rather than fuel-economy standards alone. The analysts also argue that higher lifetime gasoline expenses can offset a lower sticker price. They say whether consumers ultimately save money will depend on automakers’ pricing decisions, future gasoline prices and other economic conditions, and may take years to determine.

Environmental and competitiveness concerns

Environmental, health and consumer advocates characterize the rollback of federal fuel-economy standards as an unjustified weakening of what they describe as feasible clean-car standards. They argue that stronger rules have made vehicles cleaner and cheaper to operate over decades, while the new policy removes incentives for efficient technology and shifts pollution and fuel expenses to households.

The American Lung Association and environmental groups expect the weaker standards to result in more vehicle pollution, worsen public-health risks and accelerate climate change.

Beyond the pollution and public-health concerns, critics and industry observers, including Helper and Ann Carlson, challenge the Transportation Department’s expectation that the changes will encourage auto sales and U.S. manufacturing. They warn that the rollback could slow the U.S. transition to EVs and fuel-efficient technology, reducing American automakers’ competitiveness as foreign electric-vehicle manufacturers advance.

Legal challenges and policy uncertainty

The Trump administration contends that the Biden-era standards exceeded what Congress required. Automakers making multi-year product decisions also face the possibility of another policy reversal: NPR and the Los Angeles Times report that a future administration could restore tougher fuel-economy requirements. The more immediate uncertainty is an expected court challenge: California and environmental groups are expected to challenge the revised rules in court, the Los Angeles Times reports.
