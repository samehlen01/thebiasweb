Badenoch's £10bn missile shield pledge faces questions over coverage

Kemi Badenoch has pledged £10 billion ($13.3 billion) over 10 years for a missile-and-drone defence programme called Britannia Shield. The pledge faces questions over whether that sum can deliver the comprehensive coverage the Conservatives say it would provide.

The Conservatives say Britannia Shield would protect the whole United Kingdom, its sovereign territories and British bases overseas. According to the Conservatives, it would combine advanced radar, command-and-control systems, interceptor missiles and counter-drone capabilities. Its stated scope is comprehensive coverage, rather than protection of selected sites alone.

Conservative communications initially described Britannia Shield as a British Iron Dome, while an opinion piece published in Badenoch's name said it would be modelled on Israel's system. Badenoch subsequently said the plan would be tailored to UK threats rather than an exact replica. Badenoch and James Cartlidge say the Iron Dome label is shorthand for missile defence, rather than a claim that Britannia Shield would replicate Israel's short-range system. That distinction matters to the affordability debate, where RUSI's Sidharth Kaushal distinguishes an Israeli-style multi-layered shield from capabilities he considers suited to Britain's needs.

Coverage benchmarks

The Conservatives promise comprehensive coverage across the UK, its sovereign territories and overseas bases. Their five-year target is for an initial capability, not full protection across that stated area, and the article does not specify the exact coverage that initial capability would provide. Analysts also differ over whether Britain needs protection focused on selected sites or a wider multi-layered shield.

Air Marshal Greg Bagwell says £10 billion ($13.3 billion) would not deliver protection across the promised area, though it might cover selected strategic sites such as London. For Bagwell, the challenge is the scale of the coverage being promised: military bases, power stations and other critical infrastructure are spread across many locations. Bagwell also says a local drone launch near an airfield could be relatively quick and difficult for a national system to detect.

RUSI's Sidharth Kaushal offers a different assessment: £10 billion ($13.3 billion) would probably cover Britain's actual needs, but not an Israeli-style multi-layered shield, which he regards as arguably superfluous. He distinguishes such a shield from investment in capabilities including horizon radar and additional E-7 aircraft, arguing that the former would exceed the budget and is arguably unnecessary for Britain.

The threat assessment

Badenoch argues that drones and missiles have transformed warfare, allowing cheap weapons to threaten cities, bases and critical infrastructure. With war back in Europe, she says Britain urgently needs stronger defences and accuses Labour of prioritising social security over adequate funding for national security. The party presents its proposed radar, interceptor and counter-drone measures as its response.

Badenoch has also raised the possibility of hostile actors launching drones and missiles from near the English Channel at UK critical infrastructure.

The possibility of launches from near the English Channel differs from the assessment of military officials cited by the Guardian, who have argued that Britain has not needed Iron Dome-style defences because it relies on nuclear deterrence and Nato assistance, alongside a low likelihood of direct attack.

The UK Defence Committee warned that Britain had “next to nothing” in integrated air and missile defences. The Labour government's Defence Investment Plan includes about £790 million ($1,048 million) for integrated air and missile defence.

Badenoch says the £1 billion ($1.33 billion) annual allocation would sit within the Conservatives' commitment to spend 3% of GDP on defence by 2030. The Conservatives say the funding plan partly relies on welfare changes, including reinstating the two-child benefit cap and reforming housing benefit to help fund their plans.

Alongside Britannia Shield, the party's broader air-defence proposals include deploying Rapid Sentry to the Falkland Islands and increasing the Army's Sky Sabre systems sixfold. This places those measures alongside the shield in the party's wider air-defence plans.

Political reaction

Liberal Democrat James MacCleary says £10 billion ($13.3 billion) cannot fund a comprehensive UK-wide system, describing the proposal as “Truss-style fantasy economics”. He argues that the Conservatives should revise their costings or identify places that would not be covered.

MacCleary also says paying for the programme through welfare changes would harm vulnerable families rather than make Britain safer.

Labour sources dismiss the pledge as based on “fantasy numbers”, criticising its costings.

Green MP Ellie Chowns calls the proposal political posturing and demands cost-effectiveness scrutiny, citing Ministry of Defence projects that she says have often exceeded budgets or failed to deliver value. She also argues that genuine security requires attention beyond weapons systems, including cyber threats, pandemic preparedness and environmental risks.

Former Labour defence secretary Lord John Hutton supports the proposal and says air defence should be a national number-one priority. He points to significant gaps in Britain's capabilities and argues that resources for defending the country should not be sacrificed for other priorities.

Timetable and coverage

The Conservatives say they would seek an initial deployable Britannia Shield capability within five years, with further improvements over the next five years. The five-year timetable is for an initial capability rather than full protection across the UK, sovereign territories and overseas bases, and its exact coverage is not specified. The unresolved question is whether £10 billion ($13.3 billion) can meet the party's broader stated scope.
