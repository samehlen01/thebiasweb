On Friday, Navi Pillay, a South African former judge at the International Criminal Court (ICC), was awarded the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize. In a separate development, the Trump administration imposed sanctions on the ICC shortly after the award was announced.

Sanctions and response

Rubio said the United States had never joined the ICC, did not need it and would not allow it to prosecute Americans, calling the tribunal a rogue court. The ICC has not charged any US nationals. He said the sanctions were intended to cut off its access to resources and cripple its ability to operate.

Although sanctions were imposed, the US Treasury provided an initial six-month grace period before some sanctions measures take effect. Rubio said the sanctions were structured to give the court and its member states an opportunity to address US concerns over sovereignty and the prospect of ICC prosecutions of Americans. He indicated the sanctions' terms could change if the court and its member states did not address those concerns.

Reuters reported that the sanctions could disrupt the court's relationships with banks, insurers, software providers and other services. Reuters also reported that the ICC had prepared measures to mitigate the potential impact.

The ICC called the sanctions an assault on the rule of law and an obstruction of justice, saying they were designed to prevent it from doing its work.

The UN and EU also opposed the measures, arguing that the court must operate independently to hold people accountable for genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity.

Pillay said the sanctions targeted judges for the judicial orders they issued. She said the measures had left hundreds of ICC staff feeling under threat.

Kaja Kallas said the EU had options to help ensure that the ICC could continue operating despite the sanctions. She also said the six-month grace period offered a window for dialogue between the United States and the court's member states.

Award rationale and objections

The Nobel Committee said it awarded Pillay the prize for her efforts to promote peace and international law. It praised her courage and integrity, saying she had helped build a more comprehensive global legal order and had been instrumental in ensuring the prosecution of war crimes, crimes against humanity and genocide.

The committee and Pillay argue that impartial legal institutions and international law are necessary to prevent war and sustain peace. In their view, courts must enforce the rule of law to mediate conflicts, while accountability for war criminals and justice for victims are preconditions for a peaceful world.

Supporters, including Cyril Ramaphosa and human-rights advocates, pointed to her defense of anti-apartheid activists and her international judicial career. They said the award honored Pillay's contributions to justice, dignity and peace and recognized people who defend human rights despite the risks. Pillay is serving as an ad hoc judge at the International Court of Justice. The ICJ rules on disputes between states, while the ICC investigates and prosecutes individuals accused of atrocities and other grave international crimes.

Israel's objections to the award

In September 2025, a UN commission chaired by Pillay concluded that Israel had committed genocide against Palestinians in Gaza. The commission's conclusion was not a ruling by an international court. Israel rejected the findings as politically motivated, biased, distorted and false.

Israel objected to the commission's finding and the prize. Israel's Foreign Ministry argued that honoring Pillay legitimized anti-Israel hatred rather than justice. It described her work as politically motivated demonization of Israel and the award as a weaponization of the Nobel Prize.

Separately, Anne Bayefsky and UN Watch challenged Pillay's suitability, citing alleged anti-Israel bias and their allegations concerning her handling of antisemitism, according to Fox News. They cited alleged breaches of investigators' impartiality, her defense of another commissioner and her prior support for measures against Israel. Nitsana Darshan-Leitner also challenged the prize's peace rationale, arguing that continuing conflict in the Middle East undermined the case for honoring Pillay. “A peace prize should recognize people who build peace,” she said, while alleging that Pillay had helped delegitimize Israel by turning it into a defendant in a political campaign.
