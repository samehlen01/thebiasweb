Appeal court clears £1.7bn A5 upgrade amid landowner concerns

The Court of Appeal in Belfast has set aside the High Court order that quashed the Department for Infrastructure's approval of the £1.7bn ($2.25 billion) A5 upgrade, removing the court block on the scheme, though further appeals may be sought. Plans approved in 2024 cover a 58-mile stretch between Derry and Aughnacloy and include a planned dual carriageway within that scheme.

We allow the appeal and we set aside the order of the High Court quashing the decision to proceed.
— Court of Appeal in Belfast

The judgment removes the High Court block, but Liz Kimmins said parties may seek leave to appeal and that the Alternative A5 Alliance could pursue a case in the Supreme Court.

Why the High Court order was set aside

The High Court had blocked the scheme after finding insufficient evidence of compliance with Northern Ireland's climate legislation.

On the climate-law issue, Dame Siobhan Keegan said net-zero obligations did not automatically prohibit a new road where its consistency with emissions-reduction duties had been assessed. She said the High Court had wrongly treated the Climate Change Act as barring the A5. It had also failed to place the decision within Northern Ireland-wide targets.

The court also said, "The litigation adopted by relevant departments has been unsatisfactory and caused delay."

On the separate human-rights issue, the appeal court found that landowners' arguments did not require the project to be blocked. Keegan said the department had assessed the effects on property and privacy and acknowledged uncertainty for those affected. The appeal court rejected the original judge's finding on that basis.

The Alternative A5 Alliance maintains its objection that the proposal would harm farmland and the environment. It continues to cite earlier judicial concerns about whether compliance with climate targets had been adequately demonstrated, including the emissions impact of traffic induced by the new road.

Delivery forecasts

MLAs Deborah Erskine and Tom Buchanan say the project has been badly handled and now needs a credible, realistic delivery plan. They cite substantial expenditure without completed construction, prolonged uncertainty for landowners and damaged relations with the department. Against that backdrop, Wesley Johnston forecasts that the Strabane-to-Derry section may need a complete route reassessment, according to The Irish News. He bases that forecast on the Planning Appeals Commission's objection to its proposed floodplain alignment and on the fact that it was not among the sections identified as going ahead. He also forecasts that, even if the project progresses, its first section is unlikely to open before 2030. His estimate assumes a delayed start to construction and a build lasting about three years; Kimmins has said further legal action remains possible.

Safety case

The existing A5 is mostly single carriageway with few overtaking lanes. More than 200 side roads connect to the A5.

More than 50
People killed on the A5
Since 2006, according to official figures.

Liz Kimmins called the ruling a strongly positive outcome and called for construction to begin as soon as possible, saying families affected by fatal crashes needed the long-delayed project delivered.

Marie O'Brien, a supporter of the upgrade, points to the danger of joining the existing A5 from side roads and argues that deaths and decades of delay make an urgent safety upgrade necessary. Enough is Enough, which says it represents bereaved families, argues that rights to life and public safety must take precedence over privacy and property objections.

Funding, route and compensation

The Irish government has pledged more than €600m ($675 million / £509 million) towards the project. Colum Eastwood argues that stronger transport links between Derry, Donegal, Strabane, Omagh, Dublin and surrounding areas are necessary for investment, cross-border prosperity and regional economic opportunity.

The court ruling concerns the scheme's legal approval, while route certainty and compensation are practical concerns raised by residents and farmers affected by the proposed A5 alignment. In comments reported by the BBC, Dympna Kerr said residents remained unable to plan because they did not know the final proposed A5 alignment. She warned that the development could divide communities and affect homes and access.

Compensation is another point of dispute for farmers whose land is needed. Kimmins told the Assembly that, under the legislation, land-acquisition valuations remain fixed at November 2024. Diana Armstrong argues that this is unfair: compensation based on those values cannot buy replacement land at current prices. She also argues that other project costs move with the market. She wants the land revalued at today's rates.
