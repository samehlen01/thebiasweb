Trump was considering whether the United States would join Saudi strikes against the Houthis after a missile attack on Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport killed 12 people and injured more than 300, according to Saudi authorities.

A Saudi-led coalition also said a projectile struck a passenger terminal at Riyadh’s international airport, but gave no immediate details establishing whether it was the same attack on King Khalid International Airport or a separate incident.

Unnamed U.S. officials said the United States was already providing intelligence and targeting support, with about 200 military advisers stationed in Saudi Arabia, but had not directly entered combat against the Houthis.

Houthi disruption or control of the Bab al-Mandab Strait threatens global trade, shipping and energy flows. The Guardian reported that direct U.S. involvement could prompt the Houthis to target ships passing through the strait.

Pressure on Saudi defenses

The Saudi-led coalition called the Riyadh airport strike a war crime and said it required a firm response, citing civilian deaths and injuries.

Saudi authorities said the airport attacks had led to closures and suspended operations, while schools in the capital shifted to remote learning.

The Houthis said they had attacked Saudi airports and energy sites. Saudi aviation authorities confirmed attacks on Jazan and Najran airports.

Regional officials reported that Saudi interceptor stocks were running low, increasing the kingdom’s demand for foreign air-defense assistance.

Foreign air-defense support

Turkey and Pakistan said they had agreed with Saudi Arabia to rapidly deploy forces under their mutual defense arrangements.

Hamish Kinnear said Turkish and Pakistani forces would primarily support Saudi defenses, including by intercepting Houthi missiles and drones. He said Ankara and Islamabad would emphasize that their role was directed against the Houthis rather than at confronting Iran.

The Institute for the Study of War argued that Houthi strikes on airports and oil facilities were intended to impose economic costs and force Saudi Arabia to yield to Houthi demands.

Ahmed Nagi argued that attacks on critical infrastructure could instead harden Riyadh’s resolve to continue the campaign.

Counteroffensive objectives

Saudi Arabia and the Houthis began fighting in 2015 after the kingdom entered Yemen’s civil war on behalf of the internationally recognized government. The relative calm that followed the 2022 ceasefire ended, and fighting resumed.

Yemen’s internationally recognized government says Saudi-backed forces have launched a counteroffensive against the Houthis, aiming to regain the Bab al-Mandab Strait and retake Sanaa.

Rashad al-Alimi announced the counteroffensive as an effort to extend state authority over Yemen’s entire territory. He argued that opportunities for de-escalation had been exhausted.

Marco Rubio said Saudi Arabia and Yemen’s internationally recognized government had a right to defend themselves, citing Houthi attacks on the kingdom and the government’s loss of territory.

Disputed gains on the ground

Control around Bab al-Mandab remains contested. CNBC and AFP reporting said territorial accounts could not be independently verified.

Saudi-backed forces claimed to have retaken Mokha and Dhubab around Bab al-Mandab. Saudi ambassador to Yemen Mohammed al-Jaber said the area had been cleared of Houthi forces. But, according to The Guardian, government military spokesperson Waddah al-Dabish later said only half the area had been retaken.

By contrast, Yahya Saree said Houthi fighters retained full control of all their recent territorial gains. CNBC and AFP reporting also questioned the scale of the government’s claims, citing government sources’ refusal to confirm Mokha’s recapture.

Separately, security and Yemeni military sources reported that the Houthis had captured Turbah and cut a main supply route between Taiz and Aden, the government’s seat.

The Yemeni military reported more than 1,000 strikes against Houthi positions. Turki al-Maliki said 100 warplanes had participated in a raid.

Hisham Al-Omeisy argued that the government offensive lacked the military capabilities, materiel and force cohesion needed to achieve its ambitious objective of reaching Sanaa.

Humanitarian toll

Almost 1,000
people killed in the latest fighting in Yemen, according to the United Nations
More than 200,000 people were displaced in Yemen, according to the United Nations.

The U.N. and World Health Organization said the renewed conflict had produced mass casualties, displacement, worsening disease and reduced access to medical care.

Any decision by Trump to shift the United States from intelligence and targeting support to direct combat would come amid worsening civilian conditions.
