Agents with Homeland Security Investigations, a criminal unit of ICE, searched Rep. Ilhan Omar’s Washington-area home on October 12 under a judicially authorized warrant, the Justice Department said. The Justice Department said the search was part of an ongoing federal criminal investigation but did not publicly detail the case. Al Jazeera and CNBC reported no criminal charges against Omar at the time of the search. The search came 22 days before the November 3 midterm elections, in which Omar is seeking reelection. Omar accused the Trump administration of using federal law enforcement to target a political opponent.

Whether the timing was politically motivated remains disputed. Justice Department policy prohibits prosecutors and agents from timing investigative steps or public statements to influence an election, but it does not impose a blanket 60-day ban. Democratic critics regard the timing as suspect. But a person familiar with the search told CNBC that the Justice Department did not consider the action a violation of its election policy because it was not intended to become public. That account addresses the department’s policy rationale but does not resolve Omar’s allegation about political motive.

Reported scope of the investigation

The investigation’s scope remains publicly unclear. Unnamed sources cited by Al Jazeera, CNBC and CBS News said it concerned alleged fraud in immigration paperwork submitted when Omar was a child.

Separately, a person familiar with the investigation told NBC News that the probe had been running for years and extended beyond immigration and fraud. That person said it involved whistleblowers, documents and witness testimony.

Omar has denied committing immigration fraud and rejected allegations that she married a brother for immigration purposes. Omar’s office said she had done nothing wrong and called the search an outrageous abuse of power. It said Trump’s rhetoric had repeatedly put Omar at risk and that the search escalated those attacks into unjustified federal action.

Republican responses and possible immigration consequences

Tom Homan had confirmed an ongoing criminal investigation involving Omar in September without specifying the alleged conduct. He argued that possible immigration violations or marriage fraud should be investigated regardless of whether the person involved is a member of Congress, and that Homeland Security Investigations should be allowed to continue its work because no one is above the law. Tom Emmer also said no one is above the law but said more details of the investigation should be learned before accountability is assessed.

Mullin said removal or deportation could follow if it were proved that Omar should not have been admitted to the United States. He said any future enforcement action against Omar would be legal rather than political if immigration-law violations were proved, rejecting the argument that it would be political because she is a Democrat. The Hill separately reported that if the Trump administration established that false information had been supplied during the citizenship process, it could seek denaturalization; it said no denaturalization proceeding against Omar had been established. The Hill presented that conditional possibility separately from the ongoing criminal investigation confirmed by the Justice Department and linked it to the department’s broader effort targeting alleged misrepresentations in citizenship applications. Immigration advocates said the Immigration and Nationality Act allows immigration judges to issue waivers for people who were unaware of fraudulent claims in connection with their application, including when they were children at the time.

Political reactions and oversight

Democratic lawmakers Ro Khanna, Sydney Kamlager-Dove and Pramila Jayapal, along with the civil-rights group CAIR, characterized the search as political persecution or authoritarian abuse. They argued that the administration was using federal law-enforcement powers against a Muslim American political opponent to undermine her before the midterms. House Democratic leaders Hakeem Jeffries, Katherine Clark and Pete Aguilar called the search deeply disturbing and said it raised questions about what they described as the corrupt weaponization of the federal government against Trump’s adversaries. They cited the election timing and years of personal attacks and accusations they described as baseless in calling the search abusive political targeting. They said they would seek transparency and hold the Departments of Homeland Security and Justice accountable for what they called an abuse of power.
