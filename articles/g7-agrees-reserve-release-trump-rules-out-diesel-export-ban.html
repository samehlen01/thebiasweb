On Friday, G7 leaders announced a plan to coordinate the release of up to 100 million barrels of emergency crude oil and diesel reserves through the International Energy Agency. The release is intended to ease supply pressures and shield households and businesses from price shocks. The announcement did not specify how much would be diesel, crude or other petroleum products, or which countries would supply the stocks. Energy Aspects questioned the nature of the agreement, describing it as political rather than a specific, binding commitment. It said the headline volume appeared designed to dissuade Trump from imposing an export ban, rather than establish a detailed, enforceable commitment to release stocks.

Three sources familiar with EU discussions told Reuters that France's proposal had envisaged European countries releasing 50 million barrels of diesel and IEA members releasing 50 million barrels of crude oil. The final announcement did not provide a product or country allocation.

G7 leaders also agreed to coordinate refinery maintenance schedules and temporarily raise refinery operating rates where feasible. Emmanuel Macron argued that coordinated stock releases and more flexible refinery production were needed to bring down petroleum-product prices, particularly diesel, amid acute pressure on that market.

Implementation and refinery response

  1. The release will begin immediately. It will last four months and include a substantial, front-loaded diesel release by G7 members and partners within the first 20 days. The announcement does not identify the countries supplying those stocks.
  2. After the initial release, the G7 will meet within the IEA framework to assess whether further diesel releases are needed.

The G7 invited the IEA to monitor implementation of commitments and the measures' impact on energy security and market stability. A follow-up report is due within 20 days of the announcement.

Export-ban dispute and diesel prices

The agreement also commits members to refrain from restrictions on energy exports to one another. Before the agreement, Donald Trump had threatened a ban if European countries did not release more diesel stocks. Later, he said the United States would not impose a diesel export ban and that a ban had never really been on the table, a characterization at odds with his earlier threat.

Two people familiar with the White House process said an executive order aimed at lowering US diesel prices could be unveiled as early as the following week, though its details could change.

In differing assessments of the global diesel market, Frederic Schneider said diesel is priced on a global market, so a shortage anywhere raises US prices. He said European stocks would help the United States by adding barrels to the global pool rather than by arriving in American ports. Scott Bessent argued that US farmers, truckers and businesses should not be left carrying the burden of a global diesel shortage. David Fyfe and Maros Sefcovic warned that a US export ban would likely tighten supplies outside the United States, raise international diesel prices and hurt Europe.

Fhaheen Khan said the costs extend beyond those buying fuel, as higher diesel prices can spread through transport and supply chains into consumer prices.

Ursula von der Leyen welcomed the decision to avoid export restrictions among allies and pursue an IEA-coordinated release. Keir Mather said Britain's diesel supplies are diverse and resilient, and that people should not be concerned about shortages.

Price relief and supply risks

Oil prices and fuel futures fell after the announcement. Analysts Andy Lipow, Patrick De Haan and Tom Kloza said the release could temporarily lower diesel and gasoline prices, although their estimates of the size of the reduction varied. Ben Cahill and De Haan cautioned that any effect could be limited or short-lived, depending on diesel volumes, release pace, refinery outages and geopolitical disruptions. They pointed to damaged or inaccessible refining capacity and continuing supply disruptions that cannot be resolved simply by releasing stocks.

On longer-term supply constraints, Walt Chancellor argued that the reserve release was not a solution to the underlying global energy problem: the central issue was worldwide supply, especially oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz, rather than diesel inventories alone.

For the wider oil market, Hamad Hussain said a further stock release could produce a slight surplus if the recent increase in Middle East flows were sustained.

Using emergency stocks also carries a trade-off. Jonathan Owens argued that drawing them down can provide near-term breathing space but reduces protection against later supply shocks, without solving underlying refining and market-dependence problems.
