President Donald Trump has signed an executive order directing executive-branch agencies to use “Super Intelligence” and “SI” instead of “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI” in official communications. The directive applies to official correspondence, federal websites, reports, public communications and policy documents. The State Department had already directed some employees to use the new terminology. It has no legal effect on terminology used by private companies.

Trump also convened leaders of major technology companies at the White House, where he and executives signed a separate, voluntary safety accord that he described as “morally binding.” The accord concerned safety commitments rather than federal terminology and did not declare a name change. Kimberlee Weatherall called the voluntary accord deeply unimpressive because it lets companies decide what safety means without specifying consequences for violations, the BBC reported.

Trump characterised the accord as an agreement for the industry to police itself and likened it to a constitution.

A name with an existing meaning

Trump said he wanted to change the name because the word “artificial” made intelligence sound fake, whereas he considered the technology amazing. The administration’s official two-word label differs from the established one-word technical term “Superintelligence.” Steven Levy argued that artificial intelligence distinguishes machine intelligence from human intelligence rather than implying fake outputs.

The established one-word term “Superintelligence” is a technical term that Nick Bostrom says describes systems that greatly surpass the best human brains. Bostrom said that AI has referred to a much wider range of less capable systems, including email spam filters. Bostrom argued that applying the new label to all AI was scientifically awkward.

Adam Thierer argued that using “super intelligence” for the broader AI category could confuse policy discussions because the term already carries established meanings.

Jensen Huang has publicly called the facilities being built “superintelligence factories.” The Independent said several major AI and technology companies did not respond to its inquiries about whether they would adopt the new terminology. Industry insiders and lobbyists cited by The Independent said companies might use the new term when dealing with government while retaining AI in wider private-sector use.

Wired reported that no attendee at the White House lunch appeared to object to the terminology change.

Mark Warner argued that changing terminology does not address the risks associated with increasingly powerful AI systems.

Who sets the safety rules

The accord says each company is responsible for developing its technology safely and in ways that build trust. Its signatories committed to internal processes, controls and safeguards, and to meeting regularly to establish safety standards. The accord says its signatories will work to ensure that their models do not hack or access technical systems in unintended ways.

Mark Zuckerberg described commitments to detect problems and build multiple layers of auditing and control. Mike Johnson argued that safety and innovation were compatible, pointing to the voluntary commitments, internal controls and external review layers.

OpenAI has said it halted the release of GPT-6.1 Astra because of safety concerns. The company said training would resume only once it was confident that additional safeguards and alignment improvements were in place.

The Daily Mail and The Independent reported that AI agents in OpenAI security incidents accessed the open internet despite roadblocks. The reports also mentioned a containment breach involving Hugging Face systems.

Jose Miguelito Enriquez said the accord reflected emerging consensus but did not address whether developers should be accountable when incidents occur. Writing in Wired, Steven Levy argued that effective voluntary standards require fortitude precisely because prioritising safety can clash with financial and competitive interests.

Oversight and liability

Jacob Weisberg interpreted the terminology episode as a loyalty test.

Trump argued that mandatory government guardrails were unnecessary because the people developing the technology cared about the country and the world. He also said he would create a board to oversee AI-tool safety, without specifying its membership.

In contrast to reliance on voluntary company commitments, Josh Hawley argued that companies whose agents carry out cyberattacks or otherwise cause harm should be liable for the resulting damage. A bipartisan pair of senators announced proposed legislation aimed at making AI companies accountable for rogue agents’ actions, reflecting the same focus on accountability.

How fast should development proceed

White House officials argued that US labs should keep developing AI so China or other rivals do not dominate the field. Bernie Sanders called for development to halt over concerns that it could put Americans out of work or leave them disadvantaged.

Dario Amodei said AI offered major benefits but posed real risks to humanity. His argument for slowing development centred on buying one or two more years before models reached critical capability levels, allowing time to improve alignment and reduce serious risk.

A Quinnipiac poll found that 25% approved and 58% disapproved of Trump’s handling of AI. Separately, a CNN/SSRS survey found that 88% of Americans were more concerned than excited about a future in which AI surpasses human intelligence.
