Clancy challenges murder evidence as retrial decision remains open

Kevin Reddington has asked Judge William Sullivan for a Rule 25 not-guilty finding, arguing that prosecutors’ evidence was insufficient and did not establish Clancy as the perpetrator. The first trial ended in a mistrial after the jury deadlocked. A mistrial is neither a determination of guilt nor an acquittal. Plymouth County prosecutors have not decided whether to retry Clancy.

Clancy remains charged with murder and is staying at a psychiatric hospital while the case is unresolved. The Rule 25 request asks Sullivan to assess whether prosecutors presented sufficient evidence. That sufficiency argument is separate from the defense’s postpartum-psychosis argument, which concerns criminal responsibility.

Defense arguments and juror dispute

Reddington calls the prosecution’s case speculative and says prosecutors presented no evidence proving Clancy killed the children. He says Clancy made no admission and points to her injuries, the absence of blood on her socks, her later lack of memory and what he regards as an inadequate investigation. Prosecutor Shanan Buckingham called the new no-evidence argument “laughable,” saying evidence at the first trial supported a conviction and that the defense had not advanced that argument at trial.

The defense’s postpartum psychosis argument concerns whether Clancy was criminally responsible. Reddington says every doctor who testified indicated that she had a serious mental disease or defect. The defense says months of mental-health struggles and treatment preceded psychosis that left her delusional and unable to understand her actions. Buckingham argues that mental illness does not necessarily remove criminal responsibility. Prosecutors contend that Clancy knew what she was doing and decided to kill the children after deciding to die by suicide.

Prosecutors allege that Clancy deliberately strangled five-year-old Cora, three-year-old Dawson and eight-month-old Callan Clancy with exercise bands and knew her actions were wrong. The pending Rule 25 request rests on the defense’s claim that the evidence was insufficient. A finding of not guilty by reason of insanity would instead concern criminal responsibility. In this case, a first-degree murder conviction would bring a life prison sentence; a finding of not guilty by reason of insanity could instead lead to Clancy’s commitment to a psychiatric hospital for evaluation and court review.

Separately, the defense is pursuing dismissal on double-jeopardy grounds and an inquiry into Desronvil’s questionnaire, statements and possible cellphone use. For its dismissal argument, the defense says the jury foreperson reported that holdout juror Michael Desronvil would not apply the reasonable-doubt standard. It says Sullivan should have removed him or questioned him further before declaring a mistrial. On that basis, the defense argues that a retrial would violate double-jeopardy protections.

For his part, Desronvil, through his lawyer and in a video statement, has maintained that he had no doubts about his position and said public attention should return to the children rather than focus on him. Prosecutors warn that releasing jury material could discourage prospective jurors from participating. They also want a gag order, arguing that televised interviews, including comments about Clancy and Desronvil, tainted the potential jury pool.

Legal assessments and next steps

PBS NewsHour reported legal experts’ assessments of the defense motions. Legal expert Marian Braccia said a deadlocked jury was a classic circumstance for a mistrial, addressing the defense challenge to the mistrial ruling. Braccia also described the Rule 25 sufficiency request as very difficult because it asks whether prosecutors presented sufficient evidence for a rational juror to reach a verdict. David Rossman, another legal expert, called the defense motion a long shot.

Possible paths for prosecutors include another trial on first-degree murder charges, lesser charges, a plea agreement or dismissal. Brad Bailey, in analysis reported by PBS NewsHour, argued that another trial would be futile, saying the state was unlikely to obtain a better result than the first trial’s hung jury.

No retrial date was set at the hearing; neither side committed to one, and Reddington said he could not try the case until at least June. Sullivan did not immediately rule and scheduled further proceedings for 2 November, when the dismissal request is among the matters to be discussed.
