Portsmouth Dinghy Arrival Sparks Protest Clash and Border Debate

About 140 people were rescued from a single small boat that had travelled from Baie de Seine in Normandy and brought ashore at Eastney, Portsmouth. French maritime authorities said the boat had set out from Baie de Seine in Normandy. Those brought ashore were later transferred outside Hampshire for processing.

About 140
Approximate number of people brought ashore at Eastney
French maritime authorities said the boat had set out from Baie de Seine in Normandy.

The Maritime and Coastguard Agency said the response included RNLI lifeboats, HM Coastguard aircraft and a helicopter, Border Force, police officers and ambulance personnel after reports of a small boat heading towards Hampshire. Hampshire and Isle of Wight Constabulary said police, Coastguard and Border Force vehicles were damaged, while a small number of officers received treatment for injuries that were not serious.

Reports put the Eastney crowd at about 500. Protesters blocked roads and tried to stop coaches taking those rescued to processing facilities. Hampshire and Isle of Wight Constabulary imposed a dispersal order at about 3am on Monday, and the crowd had dispersed by about 4am. Police said they were investigating alleged assaults, criminal damage and other offences to identify those responsible; no arrests had been made when Hampshire and Isle of Wight Constabulary issued its statement.

Route and local arrangements

French coastguard officials said they had shadowed the dinghy for roughly 10 hours and that it was unsafe to intervene on the water because of the number of people aboard. The French maritime prefecture said small boats generally leave from further north along France's coast and called the Baie de Seine departure "unusual for the area".

The route's wider significance remains unclear. Joe Robertson said a landing this far west was extremely concerning and asked the Home Office whether smugglers were using new routes for large dinghies; he presented it as a question requiring clarification rather than an established fact. Separately, The Independent suggested that a departure from Normandy may have been an attempt to bypass French beach policing and maritime interception measures. The Evening Standard reported that unnamed officials regarded the Hampshire landing as a one-off rather than evidence of an established tactic. Donna Jones said Portsmouth, unlike Dover, was not equipped to receive and process this type of arrival, and forecast that a spread of landings across the south coast would become a significant operational problem for the Government and local services. The Guardian said that, if departures shift farther west along the French coast, crossings could become more dangerous because of the greater distance involved. It remains unclear whether further landings will follow.

Steve Pitt, Portsmouth City Council's leader, accused the Government of avoidable poor planning and judgement in choosing Eastney as the landing point. He said the council had not been consulted and described the narrow shingle bank, with only one route towards the city, as unsuitable for the operation. Pitt forecast that another landing there would bring disruption and put people at risk.

Political reaction and border policy

Political responses distinguished peaceful protest from alleged criminal conduct, which police were still investigating. The Government and several Labour representatives said peaceful protest was a democratic right but described alleged intimidation, disorder, criminal damage and interference with emergency services as thuggish and beyond its bounds. Emma Reynolds said people could hold legitimate concerns about immigration while maintaining that intimidation and attacks on police were unacceptable. Daniel Thomas said the blockade was intended to stop the people brought ashore from leaving Portsmouth and argued that the arrivals had to end. Robert Jenrick said balaclavas were inherently intimidating and pointed to Reform's policy of banning face coverings of that kind.

Nick Lowles, whose analysis was reported by The Guardian, said the police intelligence response to protests in Dover and Portsmouth appeared inadequate. He said Thomas had previously encouraged people to join such activity and called for a more robust response, arguing that authorities should not tolerate large masked groups stopping traffic and threatening disorder. Lowles also said the scenes were creating fear among migrants, immigrants and people of colour.

The Portsmouth dispute also broadened into a debate over the Government's overall record on small-boat crossings.

16,513
UK small-boat arrivals, 1 January to 5 September 2026
Home Office data cited by the BBC put the total 43% below the equivalent period a year earlier.

The Home Office said the decline showed its approach was reducing dangerous crossings and restoring control of the border. It cited three enforcement measures:

  • More than 48,000 prevented crossing attempts
  • 1,100 boats and engines seized
  • An agreement intended to put 40% more personnel on French beaches

Nigel Farage disputed the Home Office's conclusion, saying the Portsmouth landing exposed the failure of existing arrangements and that only Reform would send boats back. Chris Philp said allowing large dinghies to depart had left the UK border open. Thomas also rejected the Government's emphasis on lower national crossing totals, saying the issue was not whether the number had fallen but whether arrivals continued. Alan Mak said people who entered the country illegally should be deported as quickly as possible and opposed their being brought ashore in his constituency. Suella Braverman described the Hampshire arrival as appalling and disgraceful, saying residents were furious and frightened and that arrivals were no longer confined to Dover.

Farage forecast that the country was nearing serious civil disturbance and that matters were likely to worsen. The 43% decline reported in Home Office data cited by the BBC has not resolved the political dispute over whether continued crossings show that border policy is working.
