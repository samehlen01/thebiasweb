Francis Halzen has won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences cited his work on IceCube and high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin. Halzen said the award was unexpected.

“for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin.”

Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences

Halzen is affiliated with the University of Wisconsin–Madison, which operates the IceCube observatory, a project involving an international collaboration. The prize carries 12 million Swedish kronor, about $1.2 million (£0.91 million). It is the first physics Nobel given to a single winner since 1992, Nature reported.

Building the IceCube collaboration

Halzen spearheaded a collaboration of more than 450 people from 58 institutions in 14 countries. Danielle Norcini described him as IceCube's “visionary and driving force.”

Halzen described the project as initially uncertain, with success far from assured. He said very few people, including himself, thought the IceCube detector would work. No one knew whether even a cubic-kilometre detector would be large enough to find neutrinos originating beyond the atmosphere.

Halzen said talented people had made his award possible by joining a project that, he said, “respectable, conservative physicists” initially would not have joined.

How IceCube works — and why it matters

Nobel committee chairman Mark Pearce said IceCube's results had paved the way for neutrino astronomy. Detecting high-energy neutrinos can let researchers trace distant cosmic sources and investigate energetic phenomena that conventional light-based telescopes cannot readily probe, according to Eva Lindroth.

IceCube is a neutrino observatory at the South Pole in Antarctica that uses buried light sensors in ice.

The sensors register light produced when a neutrino interacts with the ice. Neutrinos' low rate of interaction makes them extremely difficult to detect. Because they interact so rarely with matter, they can help researchers trace them back to the energetic events that produced them.

From construction to discovery

  1. 2011: The observatory was completed at a cost of US$271 million, Nature reported.
  2. 2013: IceCube made its first detection of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin.
  3. 2017: It helped identify the first definitive source associated with a single high-energy cosmic neutrino: a blazar called TXS 0506+056.

IceCube's observations are also used alongside other kinds of astronomical evidence. Scientific American reported that the observatory has contributed to multimessenger astronomy by pairing neutrino measurements with electromagnetic and gravitational-wave observations.

Halzen said he was sure neutrino astronomy would deliver unexpected discoveries over the next 50 years. He grounded that forecast in astronomy's history, arguing that opening new observational windows on the universe has repeatedly produced discoveries researchers had not anticipated.

Recognition and funding

Ars Technica reported that the National Science Foundation had committed $53 million (£40.1 million) to maintain IceCube operations over the next five years.

Scientific American reported a separate proposal to halve IceCube's US funding. The proposal could place the project under financial pressure.

Michael Moloney argued that IceCube demonstrates the value of ambitious publicly funded science, showing that audacious experimental designs can produce extraordinary discoveries.
