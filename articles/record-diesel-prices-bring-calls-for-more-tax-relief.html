Record diesel prices bring calls for more tax relief

On 28 September, RAC data put the UK average diesel price at 199.18p a litre, above the previous record of 199.09p set in June 2022. On 29 September, The Guardian reported that the RAC average had reached 199.53p a litre.

Reports put the average unleaded petrol price at between 174.1p and 174.23p a litre. Cambridge News reported that the highest diesel price nationally was 219.9p a litre, charged at more than 100 motorway service stations, mostly operated by BP and Welcome Break.

Impact on drivers and businesses

The RAC estimates that filling an average family car with diesel now costs almost £110 ($145.8).

The Department for Transport's latest figures recorded 15.1 million diesel-fuelled vehicles licensed on UK roads at the end of June, including 9.8 million diesel cars.

RAC spokesman Simon Williams described the record price as a financial blow to households and businesses that rely on vehicles regularly. He expects higher diesel costs to be passed through supply chains to consumers buying goods and services dependent on diesel vehicles, extending the pressure beyond drivers.

Dr Jonathan Owens, a University of Salford expert, said the effects could spread across much of the UK economy because of its reliance on road freight. He identified supermarkets, manufacturing, construction, agriculture and e-commerce among the sectors exposed to higher diesel costs.

Anna Sharkey of MaPS, which surveyed drivers' fuel affordability, urged drivers to shop around for cheaper local fuel, arguing that even small savings could make a meaningful difference. MaPS said many drivers could not simply cut back, and its survey found that some were reducing travel or household spending to afford fuel.

Policy response

The RAC urged the government to consider further fuel-duty relief or lower VAT to reduce drivers' costs. Williams argued that, although the UK had limited leverage over the conflict and oil prices, government policy could still ease the burden on motorists rather than leaving relief entirely dependent on global markets.

The AA's Luke Bosdet called for fuel-duty relief to be extended and increased, particularly for small businesses and rural diesel users. He argued that these groups faced disproportionate pressure, while businesses might not be able to pass higher fuel bills automatically on to their customers.

Separately, Patrick Harvie proposed an immediate, Scotland-wide £2 ($2.65) bus fare cap as a response to rising transport costs in Scotland. He argued that low-income households already struggled with essential bills and that dependence on private cars was becoming unaffordable. Cheaper public transport, he said, would reduce day-to-day spending.

Chancellor John Healey pointed to the government's introduction of Fuel Finder and its freeze on any rise in fuel duty until the end of the year. He said rising diesel prices were a concern for people, businesses and drivers at the pump, and that the government would decide on any additional action in the Budget.

Supply pressures and export-ban speculation

Plymouth Live attributed high diesel prices to constrained supply following attacks on Russian refineries and disruption to fuel trade through the Strait of Hormuz. Williams argued that the extraordinarily high prices demonstrated how exposed the UK was to distant geopolitical events through its fuel supply and dependence on oil prices.

A US restriction on diesel exports remains a speculative future scenario, not an enacted policy. Reports said US Treasury officials were assessing whether a full or partial ban would work, while separate reports mentioned a possible 90-day restriction. Donald Trump said he had called for diesel to be kept in the US.

Energy expert Ellen Fraser warned that diesel prices could rise further amid speculation about a US export ban. RSM chief economist Thomas Pugh said a ban would have an immediate effect on the UK, particularly for road haulage, agriculture, construction and distribution.

Williams said a short-lived fall in oil prices would not be enough to lower forecourt prices: cheaper fuel would require oil to remain at a lower price for weeks rather than days.
