Healey Pledges Tighter Public-Spending Control in First Speech

At the Manufacturing Technology Centre in Coventry, John Healey delivered his first major speech as chancellor. He pledged tighter public-spending control and said his 28 October Budget would support stronger growth while sticking to fiscal rules. He also set out support for fast-growing firms in northern England and a 25% target to cut the burden of business regulation by the end of the parliament.

Healey presented economic growth, wealth creation and investment as the defining purpose of his time at the Treasury. He argued that profitable businesses, an active state and investment-friendly conditions could ease pressures on firms and households, and said Britain had substantial unused potential and was beginning to turn a corner.

Fiscal rules and tax uncertainty

Healey described the government's self-imposed fiscal target as balancing day-to-day spending with tax receipts by 2029–30. In describing his position on that target, he said he and Andy Burnham were aligned on meeting it. He also argued that moving people from benefits into work would lower costs to taxpayers and generate tax revenue. Healey said fiscal credibility and growth could not be separated, arguing that the rules would control borrowing, leave a buffer in the public finances and ease inflationary and longer-term pressures.

The Mirror reported that taxpayer debt was close to £3 trillion ($3.97 trillion) and that government gilt yields were at their highest level since the 2008 financial crisis. Healey said interest payments consume a tenth of government spending and described that burden as unprogressive, arguing that tighter control of public expenditure was consistent with progressive values and the wider growth agenda.

Healey declined to say whether taxes would rise before the Budget or engage with pre-Budget tax predictions, the Mirror reported.

If I respond to speculation now it will create more speculation.
— John Healey, chancellor

Separate reports and political forecasts added to the uncertainty. Economists cited by the Guardian said spending pressures and the Iran war's effect on global bond markets could force tax rises or spending cuts. The Daily Mail reported that Healey might raise capital-gains tax. Reform UK's Robert Jenrick predicted that taxes on working people would rise, arguing that the government's refusal to cut benefits, foreign aid or net-zero subsidies would lead to higher taxes. Jenrick and Andrew Griffith said the speech lacked sufficient policy to deliver growth, while Griffith argued that warm words would neither create growth nor offset what he alleged was damage to businesses and family finances.

Investment, skills and regulatory reform

HM Treasury expects investments of £5 million ($6.62 million) to £15 million ($19.9 million) to support university spinouts and ambitious businesses as they move beyond an early stage.

Healey said the government would extend judicial-review reforms beyond energy schemes to major infrastructure programmes, including transport and water. He argued that consultation requirements, litigation and administrative processes can deter private investment and restrain growth.

Government guidance on consultation, cited alongside the proposed judicial-review reforms, said there is no general duty to consult and that ministers should normally make decisions and proceed to act. It also said ministers may take decisions supported by a tenable legal argument even where the legal risk is high.

Healey said the Treasury's Green Book discount rate would be lowered from 3.5% to 3%. He said the lower rate would favour projects with longer-term potential.

Healey said the Union Learning Fund would receive £15 million ($19.9 million) from existing government budgets.

Shipyards, procurement and marine research

Plans for the three new floating docks at HM Naval Base Clyde were first set out in 2023. Healey said a UK-only competition would be launched for three floating docks at HM Naval Base Clyde in Faslane. The Ministry of Defence said the docks are part of a wider £15 billion ($19.9 billion) programme to upgrade Royal Navy shipyards, rather than a standalone project. The government expects the facilities to support the next generation of British submarines and enter service in the early 2030s.

Healey argued that backing British shipyards would strengthen national security, industrial resilience and future growth. GMB representative Robert Deavy supported a UK-only tender, saying domestic procurement was overdue because major contracts had too often gone overseas. He said British contracts could protect skilled jobs, apprenticeships and local economies.

Conservative critic Andrew Griffith alleged that the dock announcement recycled plans already made public and did not explain how the defence programme would be financed. He also said the defence investment plan had a £4.7 billion ($6.22 billion) funding gap.

Louise Haigh said the government was investing £115 million ($152 million) in a marine research vessel that it says will be procured and built in the UK. The vessel is expected to enter service in the early 2030s, replace RV Cefas Endeavour and support a national system for monitoring UK seas.

Business concerns and political criticism

The CBI report of falling business activity was a counterpoint to Healey's optimism that Britain was turning a corner. The Confederation of British Industry said firms recorded falling activity in the three months to September and expected further contraction through the rest of the year. Alpesh Paleja of the CBI said Budget uncertainty was holding back activity in some sectors.

Corin Crane of the British Chambers of Commerce said companies needed lower operating costs, including lower employer National Insurance, relief on energy costs and reductions in business rates, so they could hire and invest.

Jaguar Land Rover announced plans to cut 4,000 jobs.

Green Party MP Ellie Chowns challenged Healey's emphasis on growth, arguing that prosperity should be sustainable, equitable and meaningful rather than based on expansion regardless of its effects.

Liberal Democrat deputy leader Daisy Cooper alleged that Labour had increased costs for employers through what she called a growth-crushing jobs tax and other measures that made recruitment harder.
