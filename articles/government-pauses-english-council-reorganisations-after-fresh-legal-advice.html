Government Pauses English Council Reorganisations After Fresh Legal Advice

MHCLG has withdrawn reorganisation decisions for Essex, Hampshire, Norfolk and Suffolk. The announcement followed new legal advice. It has also paused and is reviewing 14 further proposal groupings, with Cambridgeshire and Peterborough and West Sussex to be considered as part of the review.

The proposals were intended to replace two-tier county and district council systems with larger unitary authorities. MHCLG argues that, if reorganisation proceeds, putting one authority in charge of an area could simplify governance, improve public services, speed housing and infrastructure delivery, and boost regional growth.

Legal review and elections

Angela Rayner said the review is intended to ensure the process is robust, legally compliant and aligned with the new government's priorities to rewire the state.

I want to satisfy myself firstly that the right process is in place, that it is robust, and of course complies with the law.
— Angela Rayner, government

Rayner said a new government and prime minister were entitled to examine whether earlier decisions met additional considerations before implementation continued. The review follows legal challenges launched by some councils, while others were reported to be considering action; reporting put the combined total at at least eight councils.

MHCLG says local elections in May 2027 will be held on existing council boundaries. It says scheduled elections for prospective new authorities will not take place as scheduled, while elections for current councils will proceed instead. The Lancashire Telegraph reported that the Burnham administration in Lancashire still expected elections to new authorities there in May 2027, but had not explained how this would work during the review. This reported expectation conflicts with MHCLG's national position and remains unresolved.

State of play

  • Withdrawn decisions: Essex, Hampshire, Norfolk and Suffolk.
  • Paused proposal groupings (14): Derby and Derbyshire; Devon, Plymouth and Torbay; East Sussex and Brighton; Gloucestershire; Hertfordshire; Kent and Medway; Lancashire, Blackburn with Darwen and Blackpool; Leicester, Leicestershire and Rutland; Lincolnshire, North Lincolnshire and North East Lincolnshire; Nottingham and Nottinghamshire; Oxfordshire and West Berkshire; Staffordshire and Stoke-on-Trent; Warwickshire; Worcestershire.
  • Delayed consideration: Cambridgeshire and Peterborough, where no final decision had been made.
  • No reorganisation decision yet: West Sussex, which will be considered alongside the wider review.
  • Outside the review: Surrey, where East Surrey and West Surrey remain due to begin operating in April 2027.
  • Elections: MHCLG says May 2027 polls will use existing council boundaries.

The categories distinguish withdrawn decisions from the 14 paused proposal groupings, proposals awaiting consideration as part of the review, and Surrey's separate timetable.

Costs, consultation and work under way

In Derby and Derbyshire, a July proposal would have replaced 10 councils with two larger authorities from 2028, but the decision is paused. The Local Democracy Reporting Service said the pause had created uncertainty over work already under way, including preparations for the intended launch of prospective authorities.

Derbyshire councils estimated a £66 million ($89.2 million) setup cost for the reorganisation and projected annual savings of £44 million ($59.5 million) from its third year onwards. The savings are estimates, not realised money.

Separately, in Warwickshire, George Finch, an opponent of the programme, argued that it had been imposed without a mandate and that its costs would outstrip the savings claimed for it.

In an MHCLG consultation on Leicester boundary expansion, 87% of 6,615 respondents disagreed with the proposals.

This is not an England-wide measure. In Lancashire, opponents warned that the proposed Pennine Lancashire authority could need job cuts or service reductions to balance its budget; this was a forecast from opponents, not a confirmed financial outcome.

Local leaders split over the review

On legal process, Conservative Hampshire county council leader Nick Adams-King welcomed the withdrawal as the "right decision" and said it vindicated the council's challenge to the earlier procedure. He said the council did not seek to protect the current system but wanted a "once-in-a-generation" restructuring that was lawful, properly evidenced and durable. Robin Hunter-Clarke called Norfolk's withdrawal "a victory for democracy and common sense", saying the county council had assembled a strong legal case against plans it considered wrong for Norfolk. Jonathan Gullis said the pause supported Newcastle-under-Lyme Borough Council's opposition to abolition of the historic borough. The council had sought evidence and financial assumptions and was prepared to pursue a legal challenge.

On cost, evidence and implementation, Liberal Democrat Keith House called the pause "absolute chaos", saying it was costly and would not resolve what he says is the county council's financial unsustainability beyond 2028. House forecast that delay would add costs for residents, staff and services. Steve Jarvis said Hertfordshire's four-unitary option was the "most costly, complicated and disruptive" model, and that changes affecting the county for decades required rigorous evidence and financial analysis. He cited consultation analysis that he said favoured a two-unitary alternative. The County Councils Network, represented by Sean Matthews, said many members would be relieved because they regarded the withdrawn and reviewed decisions as harmful to services and financially unsustainable. It called for any renewed process to allow realistic implementation periods and produce unitary councils able to sustain and improve local services. In analysis, Jonathan Carr-West warned that the pause risked becoming a vast waste of time, money and goodwill after leaders, councillors and staff had already spent thousands of hours meeting MHCLG deadlines.

On local identity and the pace of change, Jill Cleary, leader of New Forest District Council, said any future Hampshire structure must keep the New Forest district intact. Shabina Qayyum took a more cautious view in Cambridgeshire and Peterborough, calling a careful review understandable because creating a new council system is a vast undertaking, particularly where authorities run adult social care and children's services.

The review has drawn support from those calling for lawful, evidence-based restructuring, alongside warnings about costs, service sustainability and disruption to implementation. Other responses have stressed the need to preserve local areas and to take sufficient care over a complex change, while MHCLG maintains that reorganisation could improve services and support regional growth.
