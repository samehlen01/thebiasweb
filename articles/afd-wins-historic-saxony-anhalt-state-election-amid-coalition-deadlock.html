AfD Wins Historic Saxony-Anhalt State Election Amid Coalition Deadlock

Alternative für Deutschland won 43.8% of the vote in Saxony-Anhalt, its strongest result in a German state election. In a partial reported seat breakdown, it secured 39 seats in the 83-member parliament, three short of an outright majority. The CDU finished second with 17.2% and 15 seats. Bündnis Sahra Wagenknecht, or BSW, crossed the 5% parliamentary threshold with 5.3% and five seats. Parties falling below 5% do not receive seats in the state parliament. The listed figures account for 59 seats; the partial breakdown does not identify the allocation of the remaining seats. Outlets reported turnout estimates of 76.5% and 77.8%.

An AfD-BSW combination would hold 44 seats, above the majority threshold, based on their reported 39 and five seats. Axios reported that BSW could help provide an AfD-led parliamentary majority or tacitly enable an AfD minority government. But BSW's co-chair said the party would not vote for the AfD candidate, Ulrich Siegmund, or the incumbent, Sven Schulze, as minister-president.

AfD representative Beatrix von Storch said the party had a clear mandate to govern and argued that refusing talks would mean failing to accept the electorate's decision. Siegmund said the AfD would seek talks with parliamentary groups and individual lawmakers, and suggested politicians could change parties to help create a governing majority.

The CDU has ruled out cooperation with the AfD. This position is part of the Brandmauer, or firewall, under which mainstream German parties have declined to negotiate with or form coalitions with the AfD. Franziska Hoppermann said her party should not work with the AfD, describing it as right-wing extremist and citing its position on the euro, what she described as close work with Russia, and its preferred social model.

State powers and policy stakes

German states have extensive responsibilities in areas including security and education, and their governments participate in the Bundesrat. The Guardian said an AfD-led Saxony-Anhalt government would have broad influence over education, culture and domestic security.

Siegmund interpreted the vote as evidence that people want to recover what he called a secure and familiar Germany. If the AfD governs, he has said it would begin an immediate remigration and deportation offensive. The German Economic Institute argued that such an immigration policy would be calamitous for Saxony-Anhalt because the state has an ageing, shrinking population and relies heavily on foreign workers.

Germany's domestic intelligence service classifies the AfD's Saxony-Anhalt branch as right-wing extremist. The AfD rejects the designation and says accusations of extremism are politically motivated. NPR reported that an AfD-led government would give the state's minister-president access to foreign and domestic intelligence, prompting concern among interior ministers in other states over the party's Russia policy; they were preparing to meet separately.

Political reaction and the response

Friedrich Merz called the CDU result the most severe election defeat the CDU has suffered in years, indeed in decades. Former CDU minister Peter Altmaier called the result a disaster for his party and said it would reshape political debate across Germany. He attributed voter anger to five years of economic stagnation, a lack of job creation and the loss of traditional jobs, which he said had pushed voters toward challenger parties. Alice Weidel portrayed the result as part of explosive national growth for the AfD.

Altmaier also expected the outcome to shape debates beyond Saxony-Anhalt and affect upcoming regional elections. Economist Holger Schmieding forecast that Merz's coalition was more likely than not to remain in office through early 2029 because alternatives were limited, while warning that the result increased the risk of collapse. He judged that the state election alone was unlikely to derail Germany's defence and infrastructure spending plans. The Social Democratic Party of Germany indicated that it could soften planned welfare-system reforms in response to voter discontent after the election.

European reactions were split. Roland Lescure called the result a very worrying signal for Europe. Benjamin Haddad called it a grave European moment, said nationalism and xenophobia were not an answer to public fears, and urged leaders to listen more humbly while remembering Europe's history. Antonio Tajani called it terrible news because, in his view, the AfD opposes liberal and western values. Donald Tusk said only idiots or traitors in Poland could rejoice at the AfD's triumph in Germany. Supporters included Santiago Abascal, who called it great hope for Germany and Europe; André Ventura, who described it as a historic victory and cited concerns about illegal and uncontrolled immigration, corruption, freedom and identity; Viktor Orbán, who called it a huge night for Germany and Europe and said it was only the beginning of further gains; and Matteo Salvini, who called it a resounding success reflecting demand for political change, security and jobs. France's National Rally has said the AfD's rhetoric and policies are too extreme.

Political scientist Cas Mudde described the AfD's roughly 44% vote as abnormal and a black day for European democracy. He said a sizeable and growing share of the public no longer feels represented by mainstream politicians, and argued that neither banning nor normalizing the far right would be adequate. He called for collective action by democratic politicians, citizens and journalists alongside serious attention to voters' concerns.

Mudde warned that broad coalitions designed to exclude the AfD can become inefficient and unstable, potentially increasing dissatisfaction and further far-right support. He forecast that calls to ban the AfD could grow louder, but said a legally possible ban could prompt a major public backlash. He cautioned that, if mainstream democratic parties fail to offer compelling programmes, liberal-democratic voters may stay home and the far right could ultimately win a majority.
