John Swinney Announces Sweeping Reduction of Scottish Health Boards

John Swinney has proposed consolidating Scotland’s 11 mainland territorial NHS boards into two strategic health boards, while arrangements for Shetland, Orkney and the Western Isles remain unresolved. The broad announcement refers to replacing Scotland’s 14 territorial NHS boards with two strategic health boards, but final decisions will follow consultation.

Government case and workforce concerns

Swinney says existing administrative arrangements obstruct equal access to care and timely treatment, and that patients should not face markedly different prospects because of administrative boundaries. The government says two strategic health boards would improve joined-up delivery, patient access, capacity and equality between patients, supporting a more even standard of care across Scotland. Swinney says board reforms, targeted investment and the National Flow Plan together would create enough capacity for no patient to wait more than 26 weeks by the end of 2031.

Reports by The Herald Scotland and Press Association placed the Scottish Government's projected public-finance shortfall at about £4.7 billion ($6.36 billion) to almost £5 billion ($6.76 billion) by the end of the decade. Swinney has said compulsory redundancies will not be used in the NHS or the wider public-sector reform programme.

I’ll maintain the no compulsory redundancy commitment.
— John Swinney

Unite general secretary Sharon Graham calls the programme a “slash and burn” assault on the NHS and forecasts swingeing cuts alongside thousands of public-sector job losses. That is Unite’s forecast: Swinney has not specified how many jobs could be affected, but says public-sector employment is likely to decline over the coming years. Unite says it will consult members about the announcement’s damaging implications and has kept open the possibility of disruptive industrial action.

Dr Iain Kennedy of BMA Scotland says ministers must demonstrate that a reorganisation of this scale will improve patient care rather than merely save money. He says the limited detail available makes evidence especially important and that existing NHS pressures still need attention. Colin Poolman of the Royal College of Nursing says structural change alone will not resolve growing demand, workforce strain or problems in NHS culture. The RCN is seeking long-term workforce planning, transparency and meaningful staff involvement, and has raised concerns that savings could become the principal driver.

BMA Scotland says reducing the number of boards will not address the causes of doctors’ burnout and service pressures. It says reforms must protect workload, rest, wellbeing and continuity of care. BMA Scotland warns that mergers and wider service changes could add disruption for already stretched staff for years, while overlooking staff wellbeing would shift costs elsewhere and make patient care less safe.

Access, social care and local influence

Alongside the board changes, the government plans to give the NHS greater responsibility for social care, which is currently led by councils. It says the model would create a “single line of decision-making, accountability and funding, with the NHS taking the lead”. The government says this would reduce delayed discharges by shortening the time people spend in hospital awaiting care packages. Roz Foyer of the Scottish Trades Union Congress calls the two-board plan reckless centralisation rather than effective reform, arguing that health and social-care services should be shaped around local communities. Shona Morrison of Cosla says reform must protect local government’s democratic legitimacy, keep decisions close to those affected and focus on communities rather than transferring authority away from elected local government. Their concerns challenge the NHS-led social-care model.

Alex Cole-Hamilton, leader of the Scottish Liberal Democrats, opposes what he sees as a centralising plan. He questions whether the upheaval would improve care for a pregnant woman in Caithness or a cancer patient in the Borders. Jackie Baillie of Scottish Labour says two mainland boards may be too few and has called for a distinct board for the Highlands and North East.

Cole-Hamilton and Scottish Green representative Kayleigh Kinross-O’Neill warn that a prolonged, centralised reorganisation could distract from service improvement and weaken local provision. Kinross-O’Neill says the changes could downgrade local health services and require people to travel farther for essential care.

Wider public-body reform and next steps

The government is beginning a three-month period of intensive engagement on a new local-government structure and says no council-reform decisions have been taken. Greater NHS responsibility for social care would alter the relationship between national health structures and council-led services.

The health proposal is part of a wider Programme for Government that would substantially reduce public bodies over the next five years, including bringing Transport Scotland into core government and creating a single environmental body.

Scottish Conservative representatives Murdo Fraser and Russell Findlay say the government lacks credibility to deliver another major reorganisation, citing what they describe as weak detail and a poor reform record. Baillie separately says the programme’s promises require delivery rather than rhetoric.

The outstanding questions are how consultation will shape the health structure, what arrangements will apply in the island areas, how the three-month engagement on local-government structure will proceed, and what the workforce implications will be. The central policy test is whether consolidation can improve access and capacity without causing disruption or weakening local influence.
