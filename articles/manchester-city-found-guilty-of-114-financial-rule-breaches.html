Manchester City Found Guilty of 114 Financial Rule Breaches

BBC Sport and Metro reported that an independent panel had upheld 114 of 115 Premier League charges against Manchester City. The Premier League had not officially announced a decision. The Guardian described City as having been found guilty of the vast majority of 134 disciplinary charges. The reports differ on the number of charges covered by the alleged finding, and it is not clear whether the figures refer to different groupings of charges. City said the confidential process remained under way and it remained confident it would establish the club’s innocence.

The Premier League charged City in February 2023 after a four-year investigation, and a 10-week hearing began in September 2024. The delay between the charges, hearing and reported outcome has made the case a test of whether the competition can investigate and enforce its financial rules in a timely way. Guardian commentator Jonathan Wilson argued that the league’s credibility and commercial standing could suffer if it does not enforce its rules convincingly, while confidence in past results could be damaged.

What the Premier League case concerns

The following list describes the Premier League’s original allegations: City provided inaccurate financial information, overstated sponsorship income from connected companies, made payments that were not fully recorded and failed to co-operate with the investigation:

  • Financial reporting: inaccurate information across nine seasons from 2009–10 to 2017–18.
  • Sponsorship income: amounts allegedly overstated through connected companies.
  • Payment and remuneration records: payments allegedly not fully recorded, including an alleged failure to provide full details of former manager Roberto Mancini’s pay between 2009–10 and 2012–13.
  • Co-operation: an alleged failure to co-operate with the investigation.

The list sets out the league’s original allegations, rather than findings publicly released by the league. The reported panel outcome has not been publicly released by the league.

Khaldoon Al Mubarak said: “Our confidence and intent in proving the club’s innocence is just as strong as when this began.”

Al Mubarak said the confidential procedure remained under way and incomplete, meaning City could not explain the basis for its confidence. He also said commentators were reaching conclusions too early.

Appeal and potential sanctions

BBC Sport reported that City would appeal if found guilty. An appeal could overturn the reported findings, but BBC Sport’s report did not amount to an official decision on the outcome.

The Guardian reported that an appeal could prolong uncertainty around City and the Premier League, and that one account of the rules said it should be completed in less than 12 weeks after submission. That reported timetable would not guarantee that a sanctions hearing or potential compensation litigation would be resolved within the same period.

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy said the case should be resolved swiftly because the uncertainty is unsettling for fans and harmful to football. City fan commentator Steven McInerney made a similar argument, saying supporters could not cope with an appeal lasting for years.

Even if the reported findings are sustained, the punishment has not been decided and a separate sanctions hearing would be required. BBC Sport assessed the possible range as running from a fine and points deduction to expulsion from the Premier League.

BBC Sport also assessed stripping City of past titles as possible in theory, though less likely than a fine, points deduction, expulsion or compensation claims.

Compensation claims and wider effects

BBC Sport reported that rival Premier League clubs had sought legal advice on potential claims. If findings against City are sustained, BBC Sport reported that clubs and possibly other parties could pursue losses linked to title income, European qualification, prize money, sponsorship and bonuses. Kieran Maguire estimated that the direct financial cost could become extremely large rather than being confined to an initial disciplinary fine.

The i Paper and employment lawyer Kavan Bakhda projected that relegation could sharply reduce revenue, put staff jobs at risk and create disputes involving players. They said relegation alone would not automatically allow players to terminate their contracts.

A divide over fairness and football’s rules

The following are arguments from commentators, not findings on the appropriate sanction. Guardian commentators Jonathan Wilson and Barney Ronay argued that the league was right to pursue the case because rules must apply to wealthy and politically powerful clubs as well as everyone else. Wilson said the league’s credibility and commercial standing could suffer if it did not enforce its rules convincingly, while The Guardian editorial board argued that, if the reported verdict is upheld, City should receive an exemplary and severe punishment. Ronay argued that City’s achievements were already tainted by the reported finding, while acknowledging that an appeal could reverse the outcome.

David Bernstein argued that even a £100m ($132 million) or £200m ($265 million) fine would not be meaningful given City’s wealth and transfer spending, and that any sanction should affect league status through a points deduction or relegation-level outcome. He speculated that a deduction imposed early in a season might need to be about 80 points to make relegation effectively unavoidable.

Tony Pulis said the reported verdict meant Stoke City were cheated out of the 2011 FA Cup. He argued that City’s financial advantage affected the competition in which Stoke lost the final. Noel Gallagher offered the opposing view that City supporters and players should not be punished for matters decided in the boardroom, maintaining that the players won matches and titles on the pitch.

A Sun columnist argued that restrictions on wealthy benefactors entrench established clubs and make ambition futile for aspiring EFL sides. The columnist also argued that City had disrupted the dominance of a traditional elite and that supporters of ambitious clubs should not celebrate punishment for a side that challenged what it called a closed football hierarchy.
