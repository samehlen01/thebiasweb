El Niño may favour a wetter, windier and stormier UK autumn and early winter, but it changes probabilities rather than determining a local outcome. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) says the event is firmly established, citing strong agreement across its climate-centre network. It forecasts a nearly 100% likelihood that it will persist through February 2027. Separately, the Met Office says Pacific warming associated with the event could exceed 3C later in 2026 as it develops towards an expected November peak.

A UK signal, not a UK certainty

Pacific warming can influence atmospheric waves, North Atlantic pressure patterns and the jet stream, encouraging stronger Atlantic airflow, more fronts and greater moisture over Britain. El Niño therefore raises the likelihood of wetter, windier and stormier UK conditions in autumn and early winter. BBC Weather has warned that this could bring heavier rain, flooding, physical damage, transport disruption and power cuts. Climate scientist Zachary Labe stresses that Britain is far from the tropical Pacific, remains strongly affected by North Atlantic conditions and other jet-stream influences, and has only a limited historical sample of very intense El Niño events.

In late winter, El Niño may slightly raise the chance of a colder spell, but the relationship is weak and inconsistent; the Met Office notes that other major El Niño winters have been cold. Scaife says an exceptionally strong event could instead favour persistent westerlies, extending mild, wet and stormy weather into late winter and early spring, with cold conditions that are not extreme. He cites 1997-98 and 2015-16 as strong-event examples.

El Niño is a naturally occurring weather pattern in which Pacific trade winds that normally blow east to west can weaken or reverse, allowing warm water to spread eastwards.

El Niño is conventionally identified when tropical Pacific sea-surface temperatures are at least 0.5C above their long-term average. Met Office forecaster Adam Scaife says the current Niño3.4 anomaly is about 2C above normal. The possible warming above 3C refers instead to the forecast peak.

Met Office forecaster Adam Scaife and other scientists have described the projected peak as potentially unprecedented in modern records or the strongest in living memory. They base that assessment on the forecast peak and unusually large stores of subsurface Pacific heat that could reinforce the event. But Scaife says the current event remains below the 1997-98 peak under the standard Niño3.4 measure, while climate analyst Zeke Hausfather assesses it as the third strongest in that region.

Analysts cited by New Scientist say some record claims use short-lived daily readings, whereas standard El Niño assessments use multi-month running averages. The forecast still allows for an extraordinary peak, but it does not establish a record under standard Niño3.4 measurements.

Beyond winter 2026-27, Met Office scientists have forecast that 2027 is very likely to become the world's warmest year on record as a major El Niño releases ocean heat into the atmosphere, an effect often strongest in the calendar year after the winter peak. Climate scientist Akshay Deoras says the added warming could help create conditions favourable to a hot British summer in 2027, but that long-term global warming remains the more important driver of rising UK temperatures and extreme-heat risk.

Water pressures and global risks

The prospect of a wetter winter follows severe summer dryness. At the time of the reported Defra data, 71.3% of England and all of Wales were classified as in drought, while reservoir levels stood at 62.6%, 16.3% below the expected level for the time of year.

The National Drought Group said rain and cooler conditions would not materially resolve the water shortage. Scaife says a prolonged wet and stormy winter could replenish reservoirs and groundwater while leaving soils saturated and more prone to flooding.

Climate-change evidence remains unresolved

A Science study using nearly 1,000 years of Galápagos coral records estimated that modern El Niño events are about 36% stronger than preindustrial events. In a separate comparison, it identified an amplification of about 16% over the past 40 years. Climate analyst Zeke Hausfather says the evidence remains suggestive rather than settled because of natural variability, possible volcanic or solar explanations and uncertainty in indirect coral measurements. The WMO's broader conclusion is that evidence has not yet been established that climate change increases either the frequency or strength of El Niño events.

The unresolved question of whether climate change is strengthening El Niño does not alter the WMO's warning about this event's potential global effects. WMO Secretary-General Celeste Saulo says it could intensify heat, drought, flooding and associated ecological, economic, health and infrastructure harms.

Broader, non-event-specific resilience context

The following supply-chain considerations are not a specific forecast impact of this El Niño. An Energy and Climate Unit analysis found that countries exposed to climate extremes account for 13% of UK food imports. Economist Elizabeth Robinson warns that climate disruption in food-exporting countries could reduce availability and raise prices for imported food in Britain. She says rice, coffee, cocoa, tea, meat and dairy could cost more if overseas harvests are hit or animal-feed prices rise. These are conditional supply-chain risks, not predictions of an imminent El Niño-related shortage or of higher prices for every product. Environment Secretary Angela Eagle says households with supplies are better placed while waiting for emergency services, while Robinson supports keeping one or two weeks of non-perishable food and water where households can afford it. Robinson says individual preparation cannot replace government responsibility for a resilient food system, including diversified sourcing, buffer stocks, monitoring, warning systems and safety nets.

During an August heatwave, operators reduced timetables and train speeds, and two trains derailed within 24 hours; Network Rail said the conditions created exceptional challenges for the railway. William Powrie warns that future extremes could bring further disruption. Network Rail is carrying out a £2.6bn ($3.51 billion) maintenance and renewals programme and said its projected five-year climate, earthworks and drainage spending in 2024 was five times the 2019 allocation. Rail specialist Ioannis Koliousis argues that routine maintenance alone is insufficient. Clearing drains may prepare the network for the next storm, he says, but drainage capacity and design standards must also adapt for higher temperatures over coming decades. Tree specialist Kevin Martin says the hot, dry summer has left drought-stressed trees less flexible and more brittle, while heavy rain can weaken soil and contribute to root-plate failures. He forecasts that heat, drought, strong winds and heavy rain could cause substantial tree losses in England, with mortality at Kew Gardens and across south-east England potentially exceeding that after the 2022 heatwave; the full scale would take years to establish.

Unresolved questions

  • Whether the event will meet record-breaking thresholds under standard Niño3.4 measurements.
  • Whether human-driven warming is making El Niño events stronger.
  • Whether the UK sees wetter and stormier conditions, a colder late winter or an early-2027 cold spell.

For the UK, the forecast remains a shift in probabilities rather than a local certainty.
